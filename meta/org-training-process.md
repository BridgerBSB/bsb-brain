# Organization of the Training Process (Verkhoshansky)

A classic S&C **periodization** paper (Yuri Verkhoshansky, SSTM — **not** Driveline; included
in the HTKCH strength bundle as the theory underneath PD training-calendar design). A training
system must mirror the **body's adaptation** to intense muscular work; the **order + combination**
of stimuli matter far more than their **sum**. It argues *against* L. Matveyev's classic
**"periodization"** in favor of organizing around the **Main Adaptation Cycle (MAC)** with the
**successive-contiguous method.**

## 5 Principles for organizing sports training
1. **Methodology must follow the body's adaptation to intense muscle activity** (the fundamental
   principle). Conditions to create: stimulate adaptation to the work regimen; achieve the desired
   **morphological + functional specialization**; improve + rationally exploit energy mechanisms;
   regulate metabolism under intense work.
2. **Systematization of the training stimuli** — the load is a **single monolithic unit**, not a
   bag of means; the stimuli are **chronologically ordered with great care.** Three requirements:
   **continuity** (work/rest proportioned — the rest interval is where biosynthesis from the prior
   load develops + sets up the next load), **consistency** (accurate combination + time-distribution),
   **interdependence** (one load's effect favors realizing the next). Key facts: a load's training
   effect **decreases as special preparation rises**; the body's reaction to a load is **influenced by
   the preceding load**; total effect ≠ sum of individual effects but depends on **combination,
   succession, and the lapse between loads**; **changing the chronological order significantly changes
   the effect.**
3. **Specific orientation of the whole system** — within a phase, **all loads share one priority**
   (special strength **or** movement velocity **or** technique), aimed at a **stable** effect. Mixing
   priorities induces a generalized reaction in which different-priority loads aren't distinguishable
   + one load can **negatively influence** another. (Fig. 1: 4 wks of strength work — isometric ↑
   isometric strength but ↓ dynamic; **specificity** — strength rises for the *type of work used*; high-
   velo isokinetic ↑ high-velo strength but **not** isometric.)
4. **Superimposition of work loads with different priorities** — introduce more intense/specific loads
   **on the tail of** prior adaptations, via the **successive-contiguous method** (gradual intensity
   increase — don't apply high-intensity means before the organism is ready). **A retired load is not
   fully excluded** — it becomes an **extensive** stimulus that maintains the changes it caused. (Vs the
   traditional **complex-parallel method**: long phases of mixed-priority loads → monotonous effect,
   generalized adaptation, rapid wear-out, plateau/decline.)
5. **Absolute priority of Special Physical Preparation (SPP)** — increasing **motor potential** is the
   principal driver of skill improvement across a multi-year process. **Plan SPP first** (objectives,
   content, volume, order), *then* order technique/speed/competition prep — this **creates the favorable
   conditions** for those, not underrates them.

## Principles for establishing a training system
- Organize around the **Main Adaptation Cycle (MAC)**, **not the microcycle** (vs Matveyev 1977) —
  exploit the athlete's **Actual Adaptation Potential (AAP).**
- The end result (skill/performance ↑) = an increase of the organism's **effective work power** in a
  given regimen.
- The trend of the athlete's **functional state** indicates training effectiveness (monitor
  sport-specific functional parameters).
- Logic chain: **no SPP improvement → no significant technical-tactical, work-potential, or
  competition-speed development → no scheduled performance improvement.**
- Strategy spans three consistent aspects: **motor potential (SPP) · capacity to use it in the
  competition exercise (technical-tactical) · skill level + stability (psychological/competition).**
- The main methodological objective: order content so that SPP work **isn't obstructed** by functional/
  skill limits **and** doesn't interfere with technique/speed work.

## Main concepts for programming
1. **Realize the athlete's AAP** — the load follows the adaptation trend; the annual cycle = phases
   (MACs) with defined aims/content/volume/order. (An annual cycle may hold **1, 2, or 3 MACs.**)
2. **Use means/methods systematically** to ensure the necessary specificity of each load's effect.
3. **Maintain training potential** via scheduled increases of intensity + specificity (the
   successive-contiguous method).
4. **Concentrate SPP means in phases of limited duration** to concentrate their effect (↑ work
   capacity + lays the functional base for subsequent loads).
5. **Give precedence to SPP** — place it before technique/speed work to create the conditions to improve both.
6. **Separate, chronologically, loads with different priorities** — lets you use incompatible loads (SPP
   vs technique vs speed) while minimizing reciprocal negative influence + accumulating their effects.
7. **Exploit the Long-Term Delayed Effect (LTDE)** of concentrated SPP (see below).
8. **Reproduce the competition-specific exercise during training** (similar regimen/conditions with a
   gradual strength increase by phase).

## The LTDE (the signature mechanism)
A particular relationship between the athlete's functional state + a long, concentrated SPP load:
- Concentrate a **large volume (A) of SPP** in a **limited phase (t1)** → functional capacity **dips
  ~10–12%** → followed by **"over-recuperation"** raising parameters to **~20–30% above** their initial
  value (Fig. 5). Develops in two phases: conditions are created (t1), then the effect is **expressed** (t2).
- **The greater the dip (within limits), the greater the subsequent rise.** Too great a concentration →
  an *excessive* dip that **alters the adaptation trend** (worse, not better).
- **During the SPP concentration (t1): don't add intensive means** — the concentration itself is enough.
- **During the expression phase (t2): use specific work at gradually increasing intensity (B)** — the
  organism tolerates intensive loads but **reacts negatively to high volume** (a volume spike slackens
  the over-recuperation). **Don't increase volume during LTDE expression.**
- **Duration: t2 ≈ t1** (observed with high-level athletes at concentrated phases of **4–12 weeks**);
  tied to the athlete's tolerance to high volume + recovery capacity.
- Practical implication: **separate concentrated SPP loads from technique/speed work in time** — do SPP
  **first** (prepares the organism), then run technique/speed work **during the LTDE** (extremely
  favorable conditions).

## Standard MAC model — three blocks
The MAC organizes a **gradual intensification** of the work regimen across three meticulously ordered blocks:
- **Block A (basic phase)** — activate the adaptive process toward the required specialization;
  **concentrated SPP loads** → improve **motor potential** (functional parameters *dip* here; can't
  improve technique/speed during this phase).
- **Block B (special phase)** — develop **work power in a competition-specific regimen**; accustom the
  athlete to fully use rising motor potential + execute the competition exercise at progressively greater
  intensity (some competitions scheduled). **The new, important phase** — the gradual progression from
  SPP → competition prep; the point where the intensity curve **changes direction** toward the
  competition regimen. Gives the MAC **flexibility** for different competition calendars.
- **Block C (main-competition phase)** — the cycle's conclusion; achieve **max work power** in the
  competition regimen + max efficiency by fully exploiting motor potential. (Curves A/B/C show the
  **priority** of each load's effect per phase, not its volume.)

**Three intensity levels to grade development** (avoid over-training): **optimal** (main load volume,
basic phase) → **maximum** (special phase, without fatigue/structure breakdown) → **utmost** (the
ultimate objective, timed to the most important competitions). The MAC **deliberately has no separate
"preparation phase + competition phase"** (the outdated split where the athlete "accumulates" potential
in prep + only maintains it in competition — pointless energy expenditure). Instead **competition activity
is incorporated as a powerful adaptation stimulus.** An annual cycle has **1 or 2 MACs** (with 2, the more
important competitions fall in the **second**; adjust block durations — e.g. lengthen A in the first MAC,
shorten A/raise intensity in the second).

## How used (analyst lens)
The periodization theory under **PD calendar design** — sequencing strength/skill/competition blocks, why
**concurrent-everything stalls** (complex-parallel plateau), and the **SPP-first / LTDE** logic behind
heavy-concentration → over-recuperation programming. This is the academic backbone the Driveline **periodized
arm-care** ladder ([[armcare-bridger]]) and offseason-blend bat-speed → bat-to-ball ordering rest on. Pairs
with [[meta/skill-dev-principles]] (motor learning) as the "how to develop" stack. Directly relevant to
**establishing org training processes** for a PD department.
