# Skill Development Principles (the "how" of coaching)

Knowing **what** a good swing is ≠ knowing **how** to change it — these are related but
separate skills. Most coaches can *identify* a flaw; the hard part is how athletes actually
develop + refine skill so it **sticks and transfers to the game.** Coaching hitting is applied
**motor learning** (whether you know it or not). The hitting coach wears three hats —
**swing coach** (mechanics), **strategist** (game plan / approach / in-game adjustments),
**mental-skills coach** (confidence + motivation through a high-failure sport). When MLB
hitters name their most influential coach, the "why" is almost always *"they kept me confident
+ in a great headspace all season."*

---

## Transfer of Training (the whole point)
The question every practice design must answer: **is this making the player better *in-game***
(vs a pitcher trying to get him out)? Don't practice to get better at practice — the trap is
developing **"showcase skills."** Three transfer types: **positive** (↑ ability to hit),
**negative** (↓), **neutral** (no effect). Baseball — especially hitting — may have the
**most poorly designed practice of any major sport**; much of traditional training is neutral
at best.

**The tee example (negative transfer):** vs game swings, tee swings show **shorter stride,
more closed stride direction, more torso coil, longer swing path (distance + time), static
contact point, lower attack angle** — largely from the **lack of a time constraint** (the ball
isn't moving). Most of these are swing changes that make the average hitter **worse** vs game
pitching. (Tees still have value for specific drills — the point is *proportion*.) Why is the
tee so prevalent? Hitting can't easily replicate game conditions (no endless innings) the way
basketball/soccer practices resemble the sport — so a culture of low-transfer training formed.

**S&C transfer:** hitting/baserunning/defense are **explosive, fast-twitch, plyometric** (quick
accel/decel). Proper training is explosive + plyometric (short sprints with rest, med-ball tosses,
rapid rotational lifts). **Long-distance / cardiovascular running is negative transfer** — "do a
timed mile several times a week and you'll get better at the mile + worse at baseball." **Athletes
become what they train; train slow → become slow.**

→ Every drill/practice: *how* are they moving to do it, and does that movement transfer to a
better game performance?

## Retention — Blocked vs Random practice
Does the refined skill **stick**, or fade? ("He can't make adjustments!" / "She can't bring the
practice swing into the game!"). The lever is the **ratio of blocked vs random practice.**
- **Blocked** — environment **doesn't change rep-to-rep** (tee w/ game bat; FB machine; an "oppo"
  round middle-away; flips inside). **Good for first-learning a move/beginner** (fixed environment
  → conceptual understanding + reasonable success rate), but **low retention** — changes break down
  immediately back in the chaotic game (especially in hitting: reactive, variable, extreme time
  constraint).
- **Random** — task and/or environment **changes between reps** (pitch type, speed, location, task,
  implement). After a hitter has a **concept + repeated success** in blocked, introduce random to
  **"stress-test"** the move — progressively **↑ difficulty/randomness, back off when it breaks,
  ramp again** toward game-level (or harder). Better retention because the **CNS must re-solve** the
  problem each rep (new/stronger neural pathways — like progressive overload for the brain).

**Random skill needs random practice:** hitters don't know what's coming in a game, so why know in
practice? Pitchers don't always throw strikes, so why are practice pitches all strikes? Elite hitters
are **dynamic + adjustable** — hit off-time, cover up/down/in/out, vary contact depth, micro-adjust
late. **Good hitting = many movement solutions, not one grooved swing.** Add randomness in **intent**
too (e.g. **"around the world"** — oppo, center, pull, center; **"execution" rounds** — hit-and-run
[grounder away from middle], move-the-runner [right side, ground/deep air], IF-back [grounder middle],
IF-in [deep air SF]). **Mixed-pitch BP + varied count states** layer more randomness. **Varied
implements** = another "movement problem" (Differential Learning).

## Attentional Focus & the Intention-Action Model
"90% of the game is half mental" (Yogi). The big lever is **where attention points** during a movement:
- **Internal focus** — on the **movements of the body** (hand position, back-elbow bend, weight
  distribution, loading into the back hip, balance).
- **External focus** — on the **effect** of the movement (ball flight, bat path, contact point,
  timing, a target on the field).

Research strongly favors **external** once a competency level is reached — **internal focus has real
costs: slower reaction time, slower movement speed/power, hindered visual acuity, worse balance +
coordination** (all vital to hitting). Internal is useful only for first-learning a concept. **Why do
hitters/coaches go internal?** Failure streaks shift focus inward ("what's wrong with my swing?");
abundant **video access** feeds it (hundreds of self-videos + endless MLB swings online) → fixing
things that weren't broken → "can't get out of his own way." When reviewing game video, push two
questions: **(1) did you get a good pitch to hit? (2) were you on time?** Only if yes-yes (still
missing) look at mechanics. Avoid the **"results-based mechanical diagnosis"** trap (blaming a flaw
from the outcome — *pop-up → "you dipped your shoulder"*; *late → "you didn't get your foot down"*).
Often a pop-up is just **swinging a touch too low**; late is just **late**; and poor mechanics often
occur *because* of a bad pitch/late timing, not vice versa. The productive way to process a **movement
error** (any failed motor attempt) is **objective + external**: *how precise was contact (vs sweet
spot)? how was timing? where + by how much did I miss?* — precise objective self-assessment is "a
superpower" that powers the **skill-development cycle: rep → objective/external feedback → adjust →
repeat.**

**Intention-Action model:** **intention shapes action** — we solve *movement problems*; the body
self-organizes the "best mechanical solution" to the **task/intention** (you don't think about elbow
flexion brushing your teeth). So coaches control the **understanding of the problem**: *"hit it to
right-center," "throw a strike on the outside corner."* Ask **"what were you trying to do?"** A good
coach (or catcher/pitcher) can often read the intention from the swing. Set up the environment + the
intention (the **what**) **without prescribing the how**; adjust the env/intention if the desired
movement doesn't appear. When unsure, fall back to **three desired outcomes**: (1) **hit the ball
hard**, (2) **hit it on a line with backspin**, (3) **swing at a pitch you can drive.** "Preparation
breeds confidence" — dominating hard training environments builds confident master problem-solvers.

## Coaching Cues
Words that teach a movement; they steer attentional focus. Baseball over-leans on **internal cues**
(high failure rate breeds over-analysis). Default to cues that drive an **external focus** — a
spectrum from **remote external** (a target in the cage / a field landmark) → **less remote** (ball
flight, "hard line drive w/ backspin") → **close external** (point of contact, bat path). Effective
cueing process: **(1) Explanation/Demonstration** (show the move/outcome); **(2) Stay external**
(outward patterns, goals, **analogies** — analogous cueing builds a stronger mental model); **(3)
Focus on the goal** (ask the hitter for the intention so he learns to trust himself to solve problems).

## Types of Feedback (KR vs KP)
Feedback = info the performer receives about a skill/performance. Two kinds:
- **Objective / Knowledge of Results (KR)** — measurable: Bat Speed, EV, Attack Angle, or an outcome
  (single/HR).
- **Subjective / Knowledge of Performance (KP)** — not measurable, opinion/"feel" ("that looks better,"
  "you're flying open").

Most traditional feedback is **KP** — useful for beginners/new moves (guides toward body parts), but
**KR is far more impactful** for shaping movement + making changes. KR is most powerful when **shared
in real time** → a **"tight feedback loop"** (immediate feedback after each rep → path to
self-discovery). Example: improve swing quickness via **"time to impact"** feedback swing-by-swing,
goal **.145 s or better** — *shaving .005 s is a significant gain* the human eye can't catch. Cycle:
*coach sets outcome + environment → hitter reps → objective feedback → adjust → repeat.*

**Information overload:** modern training is feedback-rich (bat sensor + launch monitor → EV, LA,
predicted outcome, distance, bat speed, time-to-impact, AA, rotational speed... per swing) — too much
to process. The coach **directs which feedback to watch, how often, why** — i.e. **designs the
environment.** The **type, frequency, amount, timing** of feedback are critical; aim for the tight
objective loop.

## Perception/Action Coupling
The link between **what we see + what we do** — in hitting, the pitcher's motion + the ball's flight
(subconsciously) tell the body how to organize. Design practice to **resemble game conditions** (at
minimum, **perceive a ball + hit it** — why we don't train with dry swings). Examples of **decoupled**
training:
- **Perception without action:** standing in on bullpens (only tracking, no decision — add a go/no-go:
  call "swing/take," "ball/strike," "FB/BB"; even holding a bat handle + swinging during tracking);
  watching catcher-view video (often no action; **2D ≠ 3D**; clicking a mouse ≠ swinging — "great at
  the video game, can't hit").
- **Action without perception:** **dry swings** (no moving target; **different kinematics + kinetics**
  — slower, more upright, varied sequencing, fewer segment decelerations → a "sweepy" swing); **machine
  hitting** (better than dry, but: **timing mechanism cues to a throwing motion, not a machine**;
  **gaze strategy** differs — hitters track the pitcher's head→release vs fixating the machine port;
  **one pitch type at a time** → cheating/lazy tracking — secretly lower the machine velo + you'll see
  an uglier-than-game swing). *The best training is facing a pitcher trying to get you out; machines +
  "short box" mixed BP are the next best — use both machine + thrown-arm reps.*

## Self-Organization
**NOT** "don't coach, let them figure it out." Athletes meeting a movement problem will self-organize a
solution — sometimes an **inefficient** one; the coach **identifies when that happens** + builds a
**roadmap** by manipulating what the coach controls (constraints/environment/intention), **not** by
directly telling the hitter the physical change. **"Feed the flaw"** — create a drill/env that *forces*
the adaptation (e.g. a wrist-weight on the ground blocks "stepping in the bucket"; a drill that exploits
the high-pitch weakness lets the hitter discover the fix). Note: **how athletes *feel* ≠ how they
actually move** — MLB hitters often report movements very different from reality (perceived vs actual
movement diverge), so the environment-led approach lets each hitter discover his own **"feel."**

## Game-Like + Overspeed Training
Training should be game-like, sometimes **harder (overspeed).** Audit by counting **"errors"** —
frequency + dispersion of mishits/whiffs vs the game (if he hits .300 in-game, .750 in practice is off;
30% game whiff but 0% practice whiff is off). Use **game-speed velo / reaction times**, **mixed pitches**
(never know what's coming), and **raise arousal** via competitions/leaderboards/gamification (adrenaline
makes it game-like).

## Variability & Differential Learning Theory
A good swing is **dynamic + adjustable, not grooved/repeatable.** Train it with **varied implements**
(bat weight/length/thickness/distribution; ball size/weight/flight) — each new tool is a new **movement
problem** forcing coordination + exploration (a 28" bat for a 33"-bat hitter forces a new solution). The
**"aha" moment:** elite hitters (MLB/top prospects) **crush** new implements immediately (feel the
barrel, hit line drives) while lesser hitters fail predictably (light bat → early; long bat → jammed;
short bat → off the end; barrel-loaded → barrel drags). The elite are **better movement problem solvers**
— their swings adapt + don't break under a new stimulus; the lesser hitters had **repeatable** swings
they couldn't recalibrate.

## Constraints-Led Approach (CLA) — putting it all together
A **constraint** = anything that limits an athlete's movement possibilities (object behind the lead foot
to block "in the bucket"; an outside tee/screen to stop "getting out and around"; targets/cones; a
game/competition with a ball-flight or spray goal). Athletes self-organize externally (hit a target/ball
hard); when they solve it with "bad" mechanics, **don't tell them to fix it** (not how humans learn) —
use **constraints that REQUIRE the correct movement to complete the task.** "We are coaches of the
**environment** more than of the movement." Benefits: creative/individualized drill design; keeps hitters
out of the internal-focus trap; allows variability + self-discovered "feel"; protects against coach bias
(throwing cues at the wall). It mirrors how we learned to walk/talk/ride a bike — **errors within
constraints → feedback → adjust → retry**, so **failure is required.** Constraints can be **task** (what
coaches mostly use), **environmental** (weather/surface), or **individual** (limb length, power, height,
fatigue, injury).

## What is a Good Practice?
- **Deliberate** — clear goals/intentions/tasks + precise, individualized, **objective** per-rep feedback.
- **Representative of game conditions** — **high failure rate** (movement errors are required to refine skill).
- **Focused + intense** — athletes learn more when invested; **gamify** (competitions/leaderboards) so
  they're hyper-focused on the task while unknowingly developing skills; frustration with failure fuels
  the desire to improve.
- **High repetitions** — hitters need to hit, a lot, in this skill-rich environment.
