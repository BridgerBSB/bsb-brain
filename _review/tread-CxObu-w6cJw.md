# Long Vs Short Arm Action | What's The Difference?

## TL;DR
- Arm action length is defined by elbow flexion through the arm path (hand-driven vs. elbow-driven), not just wingspan; longer actions trade consistency for more potential energy, shorter actions trade energy ceiling for repeatability.
- Checkpoint given: elbow roughly in line with the shoulders at flip-up, near 90 degrees of elbow flexion; guys well outside that range get plyo-wall and constraint drills.
- Timing of mechanical changes matters as much as the change itself — one case took a full off-season/summer to refine, another (Brock Stewart) took about a year of daily work before it held up consistently at the MLB level.

## Claims
- [00:00](https://youtu.be/CxObu-w6cJw?t=0) Longer arm action gives higher potential energy; shorter arm action is easier to repeat consistently.
- [01:01](https://youtu.be/CxObu-w6cJw?t=61) Long vs. short arm action comes down mainly to elbow flexion through the path — longer actions are more hand-driven, shorter actions more elbow-driven; the humerus path is similar across most throwers.
- [02:03](https://youtu.be/CxObu-w6cJw?t=123) Longer arm action's downside surfaces most as fatigue accumulates late in games — inconsistent timing to foot plant, which can push command and stress in the wrong direction.
- [03:04](https://youtu.be/CxObu-w6cJw?t=184) Shorter arm action's downside is more bicep/arm-muscle engagement ("pushy") and a lower ceiling for energy generation through the pendulum.
- [05:05](https://youtu.be/CxObu-w6cJw?t=305) General checkpoint: by the time the front foot plants, the arm should be at least parallel to the ground moving into flip-up, not still low and not already leaked early — exact timing depends on individual shoulder internal/external rotation range.
- [12:16](https://youtu.be/CxObu-w6cJw?t=736) Case study: Misiewicz holds elbow flexion longer than typical to delay layback and buy time for a long arm action, despite unconventional checkpoints, and still throws 105 (mph, as referenced).
- [19:21](https://youtu.be/CxObu-w6cJw?t=1161) Target checkpoint: elbow in line with shoulders at flip, roughly 90 degrees of flexion; guys far outside 90 in either direction get addressed on the plyo wall and in drills.
- [20:23](https://youtu.be/CxObu-w6cJw?t=1223) Drill from Warren Bauer: open-hand throws (never closing the hand) force a tighter, shorter arm action for "stabby" throwers who yank and interrupt flow.
- [45:43](https://youtu.be/CxObu-w6cJw?t=2743)–[46:45](https://youtu.be/CxObu-w6cJw?t=2805) Example: a pro athlete reset his arm setup (using the spine more, taking pressure off the arm) over the off-season/summer; command dipped in his first full bullpen testing it before refining through fall.
- [42:40](https://youtu.be/CxObu-w6cJw?t=2560) Brock Stewart reportedly worked on his arm action for about a year straight before it produced consistent big-league results.

## Cues
- **"Hold flexion longer"** - fixes early layback/early flip-up timing on long arm actions; for pitchers with a Misiewicz-style long arm action ([54:53](https://youtu.be/CxObu-w6cJw?t=3293))
- **"Get it, go — get it, go"** (three-ball drill, two in glove) - fixes a low elbow/late flip-up by forcing the arm up under a time constraint; for late-arm-action throwers ([24:27](https://youtu.be/CxObu-w6cJw?t=1467))
- **"Be smooth"** - fixes overthinking arm position during constrained drills (double-knee/one-knee work), redirects focus externally; general population ([18:20](https://youtu.be/CxObu-w6cJw?t=1100))
- **"Stay stacked over the pelvis"** - fixes a cutting fastball caused by getting forward with the torso, which makes the arm late; for a pro pitcher with an established training history ([55:53](https://youtu.be/CxObu-w6cJw?t=3353))

## Evidence cited
- Nikolai Bernstein's blacksmith study: novice blacksmiths repeated the same swing path but mis-hit the target; expert blacksmiths varied the path each time but hit the same spot — cited to support external over internal focus once a movement is learned ([32:32](https://youtu.be/CxObu-w6cJw?t=1952)).
- Brock Stewart, named example: roughly a year of consistent arm-path work before it translated to reliable big-league performance ([42:40](https://youtu.be/CxObu-w6cJw?t=2560)).
- Anecdote (no formal data): Lucas Giolito's arm-action change via a 2-lb plyo ball under coach Ethan Katz ([15:18](https://youtu.be/CxObu-w6cJw?t=918)).

## Open questions
- The source pushes back on the blanket "inverted W is always bad" rule, arguing it depends on where the elbow sits relative to the spine, not just relative to the shoulder — contradicts the common shorthand used in amateur instruction ([16:19](https://youtu.be/CxObu-w6cJw?t=979)).
- The "roughly 90 degrees at flip-up" checkpoint is asserted as a target without a cited measurement study or sample size ([19:21](https://youtu.be/CxObu-w6cJw?t=1161)).
