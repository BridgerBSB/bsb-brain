# The Inverted W and Pitching Mechanics

## TL;DR
- Driveline's motion capture data show the inverted W (bottom 10th percentile shoulder external rotation, about 18 degrees, with the elbow above the shoulder line at foot plant) costs about 1.13 mph of velocity and adds about 8.2 J of shoulder energy absorption versus athletes without it.
- The pattern looks like a late arm: shoulder power stays low right after foot plant, then spikes just before release, instead of transferring smoothly through the throw.
- The fix is not a verbal cue alone; the source uses a constrained pivot pick-off and a catcher drill to get the arm up on time.

## Claims
- **Does the Inverted W have any impact on performance?** - defines the inverted W as bottom 10th percentile shoulder external rotation (~18 degrees) combined with shoulder abduction over 90 degrees at foot plant.
- **What did we find?** - inverted W athletes threw 1.13 mph slower on average than athletes without it.
- **What did we find?** - inverted W athletes showed an average 8.2 J greater shoulder energy absorption for a given amount of torso-to-arm energy transfer.
- **What did we find?** - inverted W athletes showed significantly lower shoulder power right after foot plant and significantly higher shoulder power just before release; total energy transfer from torso to upper arm was not significantly different between groups.
- **How do we talk about it? How can we improve it?** - coaches walk the athlete through their motion capture "arm action" report and video, comparing what they see to the target of an arm "flipped up" on time before starting drill work.
- **Drills to Help Athletes who have an Inverted W** - constrained pivot pick-offs preset a green or blue plyo ball by the ear through the drill; athletes who don't resolve it progress to a pivot pick with an abbreviated arm swing, then alternate reps with the catcher drill.
- **Drills to Help Athletes who have an Inverted W** - the catcher drill uses a blue, purple, or red plyocare ball and a time constraint (throw out a runner at second) to let the athlete self-organize a shorter, more vertical arm swing.

## Cues
- **"feel where your hand is in the constrained pivot pick and replicate that feeling in the catcher drill"** - fixes a late, horizontal arm at foot plant (inverted W); for athletes still showing the pattern after initial catcher drill reps

## Anti-cues
- none

## Drills
- **Constrained pivot pick-offs** - normal pivot pick with a green or blue plyo ball preset by the ear throughout; builds a more vertical arm position at foot plant and mitigates elbow hike; for athletes with forearm flyout or a late/inverted arm, progressing to an abbreviated arm swing variation
- **Catcher drill** - athlete crouches like a catcher, catches or receives a tossed ball, pops up, and throws quickly as if throwing out a runner at second, using a blue, purple, or red plyocare ball, minimally cued at first; builds a self-organized, shorter, more vertical arm swing under a time constraint; for athletes struggling to get the arm up on time

## Evidence cited
Driveline motion capture comparison of inverted W vs. non-inverted W athletes: 1.13 mph average velocity difference, 8.2 J average difference in shoulder energy absorption, and differing shoulder power curves before and after foot plant.

## Open questions
- The source argues that judging the inverted W from photos is often wrong because of parallax error (forward torso posture can make the elbow look higher than it is), which contradicts common coach practice of diagnosing it from still images.
- The source calls the "arm up late" explanation for the shoulder power pattern "possible" rather than established, without further evidence.
