# How Does Hip Anatomy Affect Your Mechanics?

## TL;DR
- Hip bone structure (anteversion vs. retroversion) sets a hard ceiling on how much internal or external rotation a pitcher can access at the rear hip, so there is no single ideal lower-half strategy; pitchers split between a knee-to-knee (internal rotation) drive and a vertical-shin (external rotation) drive.
- Forcing an athlete into hip positions his anatomy won't allow can cost velocity: one 5'11" pitcher lost 3-5 mph for months after being put into internal-rotation roll-in drills, then hit 97 mph once he reverted to his natural external-rotation pattern.
- Test rubber foot position (neutral, 15-20 degrees in, 15-20 degrees out, hooked) and build drill work around whichever hip strategy the athlete's anatomy supports, and only push hip mobility work when a true tissue restriction exists, not a bony limit.

## Claims
- Examining the extremes: Rodney and Tanaka drive off an internally rotated rear hip (knee-to-knee), while Fulmer and Verlander drive off an externally rotated rear hip holding a vertical rear shin; most pitchers fall between the two strategies.
- Understanding your hip anatomy: hip anteversion (femoral neck rotated anteriorly) limits external rotation and hip retroversion (femoral neck rotated posteriorly) limits internal rotation; the "normal" femoral neck angle relative to the knee's transcondylar axis is about 15 degrees, with anything greater classified as anteverted.
- Test different foot positions on the rubber: recommends testing four rubber positions, neutral, turned in 15-20 degrees, turned out 15-20 degrees, and hooking the rubber, tracking velocity, feel, and repeatability to find the best fit.
- Considering dropping drills that work counter to your natural structure: an athlete with poor hip internal rotation was put into roll-in drills for months, lost 3-5 mph, then recovered his external-rotation pattern and reached 97 mph at 5'11".
- Change your approach to mobility work: a cited study found pitchers with reduced hip rotational range of motion produce greater external rotation torque at the elbow and more horizontal adduction (cross-body finish) in the follow-through.
- Change your approach to mobility work: one athlete forced a painful end-range hip mobility stretch and gave himself hip impingement symptoms that lasted about a week.

## Cues
- **"Feel the rear hip holding external rotation as he moves forward"** - fixes early loss of rear-hip external rotation/premature hip unwinding; for pitchers whose hip anatomy favors external rotation (retroverted hips), per Ben Heller's early drill work.

## Anti-cues
- none

## Drills
- **Roll-ins** - rear leg/back knee rolled internally during the delivery to load the hip; builds internal-rotation-based hip separation and lateral drive; only fits pitchers whose hip anatomy allows internal rotation, can cost velocity in athletes who are external-rotation dominant.

## Evidence cited
- Cited study: pitchers with reduced hip rotational range of motion showed greater elbow external rotation torque and greater horizontal adduction (cross-body finish) in the follow-through.
- Internal case: an unnamed 5'11" pitcher lost 3-5 mph over several months on roll-in drills, then reached 97 mph after reverting to his natural pattern.
- Ben Heller, an MLB reliever who regularly throws 100+ mph, cues holding external rotation in the rear hip during early drill work.

## Open questions
- The author's own failed attempt to copy Aroldis Chapman's timing and body positions is used to argue against modeling elite throwers with different anthropometry, but this rests on one anecdote, not a comparison of outcomes across body types.
- The piece distinguishes a "true tissue restriction" worth stretching from a bony/anatomical rotation limit that should not be forced, without giving coaches a way to tell the two apart before assigning mobility work.
