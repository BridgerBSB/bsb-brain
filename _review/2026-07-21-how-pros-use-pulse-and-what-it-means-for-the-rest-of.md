# How pros use PULSE and what it means for the rest of baseball

## TL;DR
- Driveline's case for PULSE: workload/stress tracking on every throw closes the same measure-then-improve loop that already drove velocity and pitch design gains, but injury prevention is the frontier where that loop is still mostly missing.
- Two PULSE users, Nick Martinez and Tejay Antone, describe using workload units to set catch-play intensity and start spacing rather than going by feel; a normal medium day should sit around 10 workload units, and unmonitored days can spike to 15-17.
- Antone cites UCL failure around 35-40 Newton-meters of valgus torque against 90+ Newton-meters generated on a single pitch, as the physiological stakes behind tracking arm stress.

## Claims
- (intro, measurement thesis): Driveline's founding premise is "if we can measure it, we can improve it"; the portable radar gun and MLB pitch-tracking systems are credited with driving the velocity-training era.
- (Intended Zones Tracker): Driveline has built a real-time command feedback loop; pitchers Janson Junk and Stefan Raeth are cited as beneficiaries via a linked external profile.
- (injury prevention framing): despite record velocity gains, injury prevention is called the area with the least data-driven progress in the sport.
- (Tejay Antone quote): incentives push pitchers to throw harder at every level down to high school because "hard throwers get more opportunities."
- (PULSE overview): PULSE tracks the stress of every throw type and reports workload against a database-derived baseline to inform throwing programs.
- (Nick Martinez quote): PULSE lets him move between starter and reliever roles, plan offseason progression, and judge whether he can pitch three days in a row as a reliever or needs a day off.
- (Nick Martinez quote): a normal medium workload day should be around 10 (units); pitchers who "throw until tired" can spike to 15-17, which produces fatigue heading into a start.
- (Tejay Antone quote): his prescribed recovery days were meant to be 3-5 workload units, but he was logging 9-10 after 30 throws including 10 spins; a start runs around 30 units for him.
- (Tejay Antone quote, chronic workload): he uses a personal spreadsheet of workload numbers to decide whether to pitch on back-to-back days.
- (Tejay Antone quote, physiology): UCL tears are associated with roughly 35-40 Newton-meters of valgus torque, while a pitch generates 90+ Newton-meters of force on the elbow.

## Evidence cited
- Nick Martinez: reports maintaining a target workload band (~10 units on medium days) via PULSE rather than pitching to feel.
- Tejay Antone: PULSE-logged recovery-day workload came in at 9-10 units against a prescribed 3-5, tied to a specific throw count (30 throws, 10 spins); cites 35-40 Newton-meters as the valgus torque associated with UCL tears versus 90+ Newton-meters exerted per pitch.
- No named study or published dataset; the numbers are athlete self-report plus PULSE's internal database, not an independent research citation.

## Open questions
- The 35-40 Newton-meter UCL-tear threshold and the 90+ Newton-meter per-pitch force figure are asserted by Antone with no study or source cited; unclear if these are Driveline internal data or a remembered outside figure.
- The piece argues pitchers won't stop chasing velocity because incentives reward it, then argues data can still "lower the rate" of injury without specifying by how much or through what mechanism.
