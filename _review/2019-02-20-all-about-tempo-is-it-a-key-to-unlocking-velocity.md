# All About Tempo: Is It A Key To Unlocking Velocity?

## TL;DR
- Tempo is the number of video frames (1/30s each) from peak leg lift to ball release; most 95+ mph throwers fall in the 20-25 frame range.
- Sequencing and an early weight shift ("the drift") drive tempo; rushing a broken pattern faster does not help.
- Outliers like Caminero (30-31 frames at 100 mph) show tempo is a diagnostic tool, not a direct target.

## Claims
- **What is tempo?**: defined as frames from peak leg lift to ball release; deGrom holds 19-21 frames from both windup and stretch despite a much slower time to the plate out of the windup.
- **What is a good tempo to have?**: the majority of 95+ mph throwers sit in the 20-25 frame range; Nolan Ryan 21-23 frames, Eovaldi 23-25 frames, Kimbrel around 20 frames, Gerrit Cole 23-24 frames in high school and 24-25 frames currently.
- **Why do hard throwers all seem to have fairly quick tempos?**: quick tempo is linked to an earlier initiation of center-of-mass shift toward the target and fewer pauses, which preserves the stretch-shortening cycle.
- **Is a faster tempo always better?**: quicker than the 20-25 frame range is not generally better; the author notes no examples exist of 100 mph quick-pitches in the 15 frame range.
- **A failed experiment**: Dick Mills' "momentum pitching" pushed stride length past 100% of height (versus a prior 90% minimum) to build momentum and elastic energy, but the approach did not produce results and did not catch on.
- **Sequencing is king**: Caminero, listed at 6'4" 250 lb and throwing 100 mph, has one of the slowest tempos observed at 30-31 frames, with sequencing cited as the reason he still throws hard.
- **How do you improve tempo?**: recommends addressing tempo indirectly by shifting weight sooner rather than cueing tempo directly, and fixing sequencing issues before tempo issues.

## Drills
- **Walking wind-up throws** - throwing or long tossing out of the wind-up; builds tempo and intent indirectly; for pitchers with a slow or hesitant weight shift

## Evidence cited
- Frame-count video analysis (Paul Nyman's method) across named pitchers: deGrom (19-21 frames), Nolan Ryan (21-23), Eovaldi (23-25), Gerrit Cole (23-24 HS, 24-25 current), Caminero (30-31), two unnamed amateur comparisons (30+ frames).
- Dick Mills' "momentum pitching" stride-length experiment (stride over 100% of height), cited as a documented case that failed to produce results in practice.

## Open questions
- The claim that sub-15-frame tempos never produce 100 mph is argued from the absence of counterexamples, not from measured data.
- No definition is given for how to mark "peak leg lift" in deliveries without a discrete leg lift (e.g. slide-step or no-windup), leaving the metric's application to those cases unresolved.
