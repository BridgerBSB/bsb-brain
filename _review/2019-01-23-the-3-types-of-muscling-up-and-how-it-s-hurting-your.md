# The 3 Types of "Muscling Up" and How it's Hurting Your Velocity

## TL;DR
- Muscling up is failure to relax a joint segment enough to transfer energy up the chain, and it can happen at the back leg, the low back, or the arm, not just the arm.
- Three failure sites: rear leg still driving instead of relaxing into hip rotation at landing, low back tensing early and blocking hip-shoulder separation, and the arm overtensing during layback.
- Case examples: fixing rear-leg relaxation took one athlete from 86-87 mph to 92-93 mph in a week; fixing upper-trap dominance helped Garrett go from a 178 to 194 lb frame and a 91-94 mph starter to eventually 96 mph and a 15th-round pick by the Pirates in 2019.

## Claims
- The Kinetic Chain: a higher lateral bound off the mound correlates with throwing harder, but that energy is unusable if it isn't sequenced into the rest of the chain.
- The rear leg is not relaxing into rotation / landing: one remote athlete's pulldown velocity jumped from 86-87 mph to 92-93 mph the following week after addressing failure to relax the back hip into landing.
- The rear leg is not relaxing into rotation / landing: research is cited showing hip rotation velocity does not distinguish low- from high-velocity throwers.
- The low back isn't releasing to allow the hips and shoulders to separate: the author's own velocity rose from 85-87 mph before a rotational low-back injury to 88-91 mph after, once he learned to relax the low back instead of forcing hip rotation.
- Case Study: Upper Trap Dominance: Garrett's velocity dropped from a first-inning peak of 90-91 mph to 85-87 mph later in starts from upper-trap tensing; after addressing tissue quality and cueing, he added weight (178 to 194 lb), became a 91-94 mph starter, threw a 93 mph fastball on the last pitch of a DII no-hitter, and later reached 96 mph and was drafted in the 15th round by the Pirates in 2019.

## Cues
- **"relax the back hip into landing"** - fixes rear leg continuing to drive through landing instead of relaxing into rotation; for pitchers who fly open and push the ball
- **"hang the back knee down and in to landing"** - javelin-borrowed cue for the same rear-leg relaxation fault
- **"throw loose and quick"** - reframes "throw hard" to fix arm overtensing when an athlete is told to give max effort
- **"imagine the ball is on a string, and that string is your arm"** - fixes a tense arm action at layback/release by shifting focus to the pec accelerating the string rather than the hand or tricep

## Anti-cues
- **"drop and drive"** - taught to cue a downward first move off the rubber; actually mischaracterizes elite lower-half mechanics, which begin with a drift, not a downward move; per Ben Brewster
- **"tall and fall"** - taught to cue an upright delivery with minimal back leg involvement; actually mischaracterizes elite mechanics, which use a drive/pulse phase before relaxing into rotation; per Ben Brewster

## Drills
- **Pulldowns (turn and burn footwork)** - removes the drift and drive/pulse phases to isolate the relaxation/rotational transfer phase; builds transferring linear energy into rotation; for pitchers who muscle up the back leg through landing
- **Javelin rocker drill** - modified rocker starting with the arm up at landing, thrown on a line or slight downhill, elbow at 90 degrees; work up to a 2 lb ball at low effort, bulk of reps at 7 oz to 1 lb; builds feeling the back hip relax and energy wind up the chain
- **Med ball hip-pop throw** - 2 lb ball, focus on relaxing the back hip down and in to pop the upper half through while holding the upper half closed; builds hip-to-shoulder energy transfer and reflexive front-leg bracing

## Evidence cited
- Lateral bound off the mound correlates with throwing velocity (cited study, no effect size given).
- Named athlete "Garrett": 86-87 to 92-93 mph on pulldowns in one week after a cueing fix; separately, 178 to 194 lb, 91-94 mph starter, 93 mph on the last pitch of a DII no-hitter, later 96 mph and drafted 15th round by the Pirates in 2019.
- Author Ben Brewster's own case: 85-87 mph before a low-back injury, 88-91 mph after, attributed to learning to relax the low back.
- Named athlete Mike Shawaryn cited as an example of an athlete who is already loose enough (result: reached the majors), no velocity numbers given.

## Open questions
- The source claims an individual-specific "optimal tension" level exists but offers no way to measure it beyond trial and error with long toss and radar readings.
- The source asserts hip rotation velocity does not distinguish low- from high-velocity throwers without citing the study directly.
