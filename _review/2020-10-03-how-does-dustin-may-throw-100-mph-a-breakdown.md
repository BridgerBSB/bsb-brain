# How Does Dustin May Throw 100 MPH? A Breakdown

## TL;DR
- Breaks down Dustin May's mechanics (leg lift, rotational hinge, pelvic load/unload, arm action, timing) to explain how he sits over 98 mph with 10 inches of horizontal sinker movement.
- Core model: keep the pelvis stacked and level (tilt under about 10 degrees) while riding it down the mound, then convert that linear energy into rotation instead of forcing the hip through or pushing off the rubber.
- May reportedly gained 5-7 mph since high school mainly by adding a sustained linear move, not just size.

## Claims
- [00:00](https://youtu.be/JsfstesnEZ0?t=0) May averages over 98 mph with a sinker carrying 10 inches of horizontal movement; drafted in the third round out of high school at age 18.
- [01:01](https://youtu.be/JsfstesnEZ0?t=61) Pitch mix: sinker about 50% of the time, cutter (up to 96 mph) about 25%, changeup/curveball/four-seam splitting the remaining 25%.
- [02:01](https://youtu.be/JsfstesnEZ0?t=121) At peak leg lift, elite throwers keep head, torso, pelvis, and heel in a straight line ("the stack"); lower-level throwers lose it by hunching forward or leaning back.
- [09:08](https://youtu.be/JsfstesnEZ0?t=548) In high school May threw 91-95 mph without a sustained linear move, relying more on rotation and arm; he has since gained 5-7 mph, credited largely to developing that linear ride down the mound.
- [09:08](https://youtu.be/JsfstesnEZ0?t=548) General rule: pelvis should not tilt more than about 10 degrees during the forward move, versus a 30-45 degree hike common in slower throwers; this fault shows up in over 50% of throwers under 90 mph.
- [12:12](https://youtu.be/JsfstesnEZ0?t=732) The pelvic unload converts linear kinetic energy into rotational energy, which transfers up the spine segment by segment (the "spinal engine" concept).
- [17:19](https://youtu.be/JsfstesnEZ0?t=1039) The unload is initiated by the pelvis rotating over a femur already loaded into internal rotation during the linear move, not by actively slamming the back knee down.
- [23:22](https://youtu.be/JsfstesnEZ0?t=1402) At ball release, elite throwers show the elbow roughly in line with the shoulder (acromial) line through wrist and ball; a bent or forward elbow at release signals pushing rather than pulling.
- [27:37](https://youtu.be/JsfstesnEZ0?t=1646) Tread states it has coached over 1,500 amateur and 500+ pro pitchers, with 28 drafted in the past three years and over a dozen big leaguers.

## Cues
- **"Float the arm into position"** - fixes bringing the arm into the throw too soon (hijacking energy flow from the lower half); for pitchers who tense or stab the arm early
- **"Relax, release"** - fixes actively forcing or blocking pelvic rotation with the back hip; for pitchers who try to yank the pelvis through
- **"Throw hard easy"** - fixes muscling the arm through warmup, long toss, and catch play instead of letting the lower half generate the energy; for all pitchers

## Anti-cues
- **"Slam the knee down"** - taught to fix pelvic unload/hip rotation timing; actually causes no meaningful energy transfer into the pelvis, since knee action alone isn't what drives the rotation; per Tread/Ben Brewster
- **"Push off the rubber as hard as you can"** (paired with triple extension toward the target) - taught to fix linear drive for velocity; actually causes no gain in rotational energy transfer and doesn't correspond to throwing harder; per Tread/Ben Brewster
- **"Rotate your pelvis as fast as possible"** - taught to fix slow hip rotation; actually causes the back hip to be forced through as a block, which Ben Brewster says led to his own low back injuries in high school and college; per Ben Brewster

## Drills
- **Long toss with delayed arm entry** - throw progressively farther using only the lower half and torso, delaying arm effort until near max distance; builds sequencing so the arm enters the throw late and captures rather than generates energy; for pitchers who bring the arm into the throw too early

## Evidence cited
- Dustin May, high school (91-95 mph, no sustained linear move) vs. current (98+ mph) side-by-side video comparison.
- Billy Wagner (named, per author's account) attributed learning to throw 100 mph to practicing "throw hard easy" during warmups and long toss.
- Tread's internal coaching totals: 1,500+ amateur, 500+ pro pitchers, 28 drafted in three years, 12+ big leaguers.

## Open questions
- The "pelvis tilt under 10 degrees" rule and the ">50% of sub-90 mph throwers" figure are stated without a cited method or dataset.
- The claim that slamming the knee down transfers no energy into the pelvis contradicts a cue commonly taught elsewhere without addressing that conflict directly.
