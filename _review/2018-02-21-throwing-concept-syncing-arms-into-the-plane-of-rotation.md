# Throwing Concept: Syncing Arms Into The Plane Of Rotation

## TL;DR
- Arm slot doesn't matter as much as syncing both elbows into the plane of shoulder rotation, which is set by the pitcher's own spine angle at that instant.
- Names two faults that break the sync: short-arming (elbow rises above shoulder height at landing) and dragging the arm (elbow stays low and lags behind rotation).
- Frames the old-school teaching of a firm front side and forward-flexed trunk finish, used to "throw strikes," as a direct cause of lost velocity because it negates trunk rotation.

## Claims
- [00:15](https://youtu.be/OZpD8SQy0hY?t=15) There are two parts to the concept: first, rotating around the spine at all; second, syncing the arms into that plane of rotation.
- [00:15](https://youtu.be/OZpD8SQy0hY?t=15) The plane of rotation is whatever the spine angle is at that moment - upright spine gives a near-vertical plane, spinal tilt shifts the whole plane with it.
- [01:16](https://youtu.be/OZpD8SQy0hY?t=76) Tim Lincecum and Sandy Koufax are cited as examples of heavy trunk extension still rotating perpendicular to the spine.
- [01:16](https://youtu.be/OZpD8SQy0hY?t=76) A common older teaching pattern - firm glove side, push the ball to the target, forward-flex the trunk at release to "throw strikes" - negates rotation around the spine and caps velocity.
- [02:18](https://youtu.be/OZpD8SQy0hY?t=138) Once the plane is established, both elbows need to sit in that plane during rotation; Max Scherzer and Pedro Martinez (lower slot) are given as examples of elbows lined up with the shoulders.
- [02:18](https://youtu.be/OZpD8SQy0hY?t=138) Fault: short-arming, where the elbow rises above shoulder height at landing, which blocks full transfer of rotational energy into the ball.
- [03:20](https://youtu.be/OZpD8SQy0hY?t=200) Fault: dragging the arm, where the elbow stays down at landing and is no longer in the shoulder plane once rotation begins, so it drags behind.
- Description: cites Paul Nyman's article "Exactly What Are Effective Throwing Mechanics?" (fangraphs.com) as the origin of this concept from the 1990s/early 2000s.
