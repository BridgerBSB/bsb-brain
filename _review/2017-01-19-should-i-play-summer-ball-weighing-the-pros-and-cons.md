# Should I Play Summer Ball? Weighing the Pros and Cons

## TL;DR
- College coaches need wins now, and many pitchers chasing pro ball need offseason time to build raw stuff instead; the two interests conflict.
- The right call depends on where a pitcher's velocity sits relative to next-level need: play if you're within a couple mph and refining command, train instead if you're 3-5+ mph short.
- The author's own biggest velocity jump came at age 21-22, the one college summer he skipped competing to train.

## Claims
- A Conflict of Interest: college coaches are incentivized toward wins and in-season polish (strike-throwing, composure, off-speed refinement), while pro-track players need offseason time for stuff development, and those goals conflict.
- Scenario #1: Welcome to college: freshmen without a college inning base can still benefit from 2-3 months of summer reps for composure and confidence, even while stuff-development lags; the author calls this a toss-up.
- Scenario #2: Spring innings eater: a pitcher who threw 40+ spring innings but sits 3-5+ mph below next-level need should skip summer ball and train; the author argues refined 88 mph arms don't draw scouting interest.
- Scenario #3: Unpolished prospect: a pitcher within a couple mph of target velocity, working on command or an off-speed pitch, should play in a competitive summer league (e.g. Cape Cod) to refine what he already has.
- Going Pro: are we selling a false dream?: argues coaches should not pressure a player out of pursuing a pro dream or tell him he isn't capable, even if the odds are long; bench or cut him instead of talking him out of the goal.
- Wrapping it up: the author states his own largest velocity gain came between ages 21 and 22, in the only college summer he took off from competing to train.

## Evidence cited
- Kyle Hendricks and Greg Maddux cited as pitchers who reached the majors sitting below the velocity norm only because they had already thrown harder (around 93 mph) earlier and earned the right to pitch with below-average velo through command/movement.
- Bartolo Colon cited as an example of a pitcher who now sits 88-91 mph but threw upper-90s velocity when he first entered the league.

## Open questions
- The 3-5+ mph threshold for "skip summer ball and train" is asserted as a rule of thumb without any supporting data.
- The claim that most pitching coaches "scoff" at players prioritizing raw stuff over refinement is stated without evidence.
