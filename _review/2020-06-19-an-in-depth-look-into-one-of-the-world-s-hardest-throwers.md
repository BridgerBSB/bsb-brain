# An In-Depth Look Into One of the World's Hardest Throwers (part 1)

## TL;DR
- Pre-draft testing of Nate Pearson (245 lbs, then topping out at 94 mph) shows middling raw scores on most tests but top-of-team results once body weight is built into a power or energy formula.
- Example: lateral jump 5963 W against a team average of 4016 W.
- The author's conclusion is that power matters, and body-weight-adjusted scores show it better than raw distance or time.

## Claims
- [Vertical Jumping] His 26.3" vertical ranked 6th on the team and sat below published 2002 pro scores. Adjusted for 245 lbs, it works out to 11503 W, well above both groups.
- [Broad Jump] A 9'2" broad jump placed near the top of the team. By the Mann peak power formula he produced 6491 W against a team average of 4881 W.
- [Broad Jump] His triple jump average (118.5") exceeded his broad jump (110"). The author reads this as adequate leg strength and tendon elasticity.
- [Lateral Jump] The lateral jump has the highest correlation to throwing velocity of the tests given, because it is specific to throwing. He was in the top 25% raw and far ahead once weight was added: 5963 W against 4016 W.
- [Sprinting] His 60 yard time of 7.12 s was 8th on the team. Scaled for body weight it equals 3345 J, which a 180 lb athlete would need a 6.1 to match.
- [Sprinting] His 10 yard dash of 1.78 s ranked 21st of 35 and his 5-10-5 of 4.8 s ranked 14th. Both moved to the top once weight was factored in.
- [Medicine Ball Throws] He ranked #1 on the backward throw (6 lb ball, 73.5 ft against a team average of 56.6 ft) and #4 on the forward throw (49 ft against 43 ft).
- [Medicine Ball Throws] The backward toss is the better predictor of throwing velocity of the two med ball throws.
- [Intro] The testing was a snapshot at 94 mph. He later touched 100 mph in late November and threw 102 mph in front of scouts before the draft. The author credits Nate's work and the college coaching staff, not the program.
- [Conclusion] Power matters a lot, and adding body weight to existing formulas shows the power an athlete can produce.

## Evidence cited
- Nate Pearson testing at the College of Central Florida, 2017-18 NJACC season: vertical, broad, triple and lateral jumps, 60 yd, 10 yd, 5-10-5, and 6 lb med ball throws, compared with a 35-player team.
- Published 2002 professional player jump scores, and published pro-baseball data on the 10 yd and pro-agility tests.
- Long jump peak power formula from Dr. Bryan Mann: -2722 + 43.03 * body weight (kg) + 1423 * jump distance (m).

## Open questions
- The author used the vertical jump power formula for the lateral jump and, initially, the broad jump, and says himself "this isn't good science." The 3345 J sprint figure is kinetic energy, yet the article calls it power.
- This is one athlete at one time point, mostly tested before his velocity peak. The article does not show that high body-weight-adjusted power explains his 100+ mph, and part 2 (mechanics) is not covered here.
