# Is the vertical slider the next big thing in pitch design?

## TL;DR
- Driveline defines a "vertical slider": gyro-dominant, within 10 mph of the pitcher's fastball, 4 inches or less of horizontal break, with 6-9 inches of downward break around 84 mph.
- It has held the No. 1 whiff rate among all breaking ball shapes every year since 2015, and MLB usage is climbing (0.66% in 2015 to 1.21% in 2026).
- Driveline's pitching director argues it won't decay the way the sweeper did, since it isn't a visually extreme shape - but that's his opinion, not data.

## Claims
- (intro): Driveline's definition requires within 10 mph of the fastball, gyro-dominant spin with just enough tilt to convert to depth, 4 inches or less of horizontal movement, and downward break (distinguishing it from a gyro/bullet slider, which rides).
- Not New. Newly Scalable.: MLB vertical slider usage went from 0.66% (2015) to 1.21% (2026); whiff rate has stayed in the high-30s to low-40s range across that span (peak 41.0% in 2019).
- Not New. Newly Scalable.: Driveline's Connor White says 2026 is the highest adoption rate yet, at roughly 1% of pitches landing in the vertical-slider window.
- Why the Shape Works: the pitch has had the No. 1 whiff rate among all breaking ball shapes every year since 2015, per Driveline's analysis.
- Why the Shape Works: White and Jack Lambert found the five highest-whiff movement profiles are all gyro breaking balls under 3 inches IVB and under 3 inches horizontal break; the top shape (vertical slider) adds 6-9 inches of drop near 84 mph.
- The Sweeper Warning: sweeper usage rose 0.7% (2018) to 8.2% (this season), more than 11x, while whiff rate fell from 38% to 30%, an 8-point drop.
- How Easy Is It to Adopt?: White says pitchers who favor supination shapes are currently the most likely to throw it, but calls that "not a hard rule" - a pitcher could get arm-side movement and still generate the drop-and-velocity combination.
- Should There Be Urgency to Be an Early Adopter?: White expects slower erosion than the sweeper because the vertical slider sits in the middle of the movement-velocity plot rather than at a visually distinct extreme (his sweeper example: 19 inches of horizontal break at 86 mph).

## Evidence cited
- Driveline's internal usage/whiff-rate tracking for the vertical slider, 2015-2026, and for the sweeper, 2018-2026 (tables in article).
- Named pitchers cited as current or recent throwers: Griffin Canning (grades it his best pitch, 88 mph back-foot example), Clay Holmes, Luke Jackson, Andrew Alvarez.
- Canning quote: hitters tell him "it's like a cue ball. They cannot see any spin."

## Open questions
- White's claim that the vertical slider will erode more slowly than the sweeper is stated as belief, not supported by a study or projection - worth revisiting once more seasons of usage data exist.
- White acknowledges the pitch-shape boundaries are fuzzy ("where a sweeper turns into a slider, or where a splitter turns into a fork") but the article doesn't resolve where Driveline draws that line beyond the stated 4-inch horizontal cutoff.
