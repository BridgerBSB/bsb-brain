# "Rate of Force Development?"

## TL;DR
- Rate of force development (ROFD) is the speed at which force is produced, not just how much force a lifter can make.
- The author illustrates ROFD with a hypothetical 500-lb deadlift: a lifter with faster ROFD locks out in 4 seconds versus a slower lifter who fails before lockout.
- Recommends Westside-style dynamic effort work (more sets, fewer reps, more speed, less rest) alongside max-strength work to build ROFD.

## Claims
- **Rate of Force Development** - ROFD is measured in newtons per second (or newton-metres per second) and describes how fast force is produced, not the ceiling of force output.
- **Rate of Force Development** - Hypothetical example: two lifters can both produce 500 lbs of force on a deadlift; the faster-ROFD lifter gets the bar moving in 2 seconds and locks out at 4 seconds, while the slower lifter takes 4 seconds to break the floor and 6 seconds to reach the knees, failing before lockout.
- **Rate of Force Development** - Cites Louie Simmons' Westside method: training dynamic effort (power/ROFD) alongside max effort (typically 2-4 sets of 4-6 reps near-maximal) by raising sets, lowering reps, and increasing bar speed.
- **Rate of Force Development** - Author's own example: a 360-lb 5RM back squat could be trained as 3x5 at 360 lbs (volume) or swapped for 10x3 at 225 lbs box squats to an 11-inch box, resting 60-90 seconds between sets.
- **Rate of Force Development** - Claims this dynamic-effort work "carries over" to heavy maximal-strength lifting, though no data is given for that transfer.

## Drills
- **Box squat (dynamic effort)** - sit to an 11-inch box below parallel, explode up on contact, 10 sets of 3 reps at 225 lbs (vs. a 360-lb 5RM), 60-90 seconds rest between sets; builds rate of force development for carryover to max-effort lifting.

## Evidence cited
- Pyrros Dimas (Greece), 1996 Olympic weightlifting gold medalist, referenced as a visual example of ROFD; no data given, only cited as a demonstration.

## Open questions
- The two-lifter deadlift comparison is a hypothetical illustration, not measured data; the claim that dynamic-effort work "carries over" to max-effort strength is asserted without a study or numbers to support it.
