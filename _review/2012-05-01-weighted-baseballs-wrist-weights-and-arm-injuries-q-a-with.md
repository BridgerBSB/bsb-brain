# Weighted Baseballs, Wrist Weights, and Arm Injuries - Q&A with drivelinebaseball.com (May 1st)

## TL;DR
- Weighted baseball throws are short step-behinds thrown into a net at 20-30 feet, not at a target 60-90 feet away, so the pitcher never tries to locate the ball; it's called a throwing program, not a pitching program.
- Wrist weights train the posterior shoulder and pronator teres eccentrically, building "the brakes of the arm" so it can decelerate the ball safely, a theory traced to Dr. Mike Marshall.
- Driveline attributes its record of zero elbow injuries to biomechanics lab testing plus fitness work on the medial forearm muscles that help protect the UCL; the humerus internally rotates at over 7000 degrees per second during a pitch.

## Claims
- [01:03](https://youtu.be/Uy5i4piUtH0?t=63) Long toss is 24-36 max-effort reps for distance, then compressed to on-line throws before weighted ball work.
- [01:03](https://youtu.be/Uy5i4piUtH0?t=63) Weighted baseballs are thrown as step-behinds into a canvas net or backstop at 20-30 feet, never from 60-90 feet, so the pitcher isn't trying to locate the ball; it's a throwing program, not a pitching program.
- [03:10](https://youtu.be/Uy5i4piUtH0?t=190) Wrist weights were developed by physical therapists for jumping/rehab and popularized in baseball by Dr. Mike Marshall.
- [03:10](https://youtu.be/Uy5i4piUtH0?t=190) Wrist weights train the pronator teres and posterior shoulder eccentrically to build "the brakes of the arm"; bigger brakes allow better acceleration.
- [04:11](https://youtu.be/Uy5i4piUtH0?t=251) Dr. Murray Maitland (director of rehabilitative medicine, University of Washington) biomechanically analyzed Marshall's pitchers and said Marshall trained them eccentrically, which most programs (focused on weighted baseballs) don't do.
- [05:12](https://youtu.be/Uy5i4piUtH0?t=312) Tennis players rarely suffer serious shoulder injuries (mostly bursitis/overuse) compared to pitchers, attributed to lower deceleration force than releasing a baseball.
- [06:12](https://youtu.be/Uy5i4piUtH0?t=372) Driveline reports no elbow injuries among its athletes, including pitchers with pre-existing elbow injuries, attributed to biomechanics lab/high-speed camera testing plus fitness training.
- [07:17](https://youtu.be/Uy5i4piUtH0?t=437) The humerus internally rotates at over 7000 degrees per second during a pitch, producing large deceleration and compression force on the elbow.
- [08:20](https://youtu.be/Uy5i4piUtH0?t=500) States there is research (referenced to Driveline's own research page, no specific study named) showing the aponeurosis and pronator/flexor mass muscles help absorb elbow load and protect the UCL.
- [09:20](https://youtu.be/Uy5i4piUtH0?t=560) Michael Pineda case: forcing a changeup pushed his mechanics from rotational to linear, dropping fastball velocity from the upper 90s to 90 despite a clean pre-signing MRI.
