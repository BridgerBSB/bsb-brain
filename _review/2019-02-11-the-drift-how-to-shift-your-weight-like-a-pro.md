# The "Drift" - How to Shift Your Weight Like a Pro

## TL;DR
- Tread's Ben Brewster names "the drift": a forward weight shift during leg lift, moving the center of mass away from the back leg before the active drive phase, which he says most of the hardest throwers do without being taught it.
- Brewster reports a 3 mph jump in average velocity on his first attempt at an aggressive, Lincecum-style weight shift, and the gain held once healthy; results are not universal, one athlete's velocity dropped slightly when the drift was added on top of a delivery that was already working.
- He gives three cues (lean into the leg lift, shift the balance point 4-6 inches toward the plate, stay inside the back foot) and one drill (step-back windup), and restricts the concept to pitchers who have already fixed segmentation, arm timing, and front-leg bracing.

## Claims
- [00:22](https://youtu.be/NHt_fvKcXJ8?t=22) The drift is defined as the initial move during leg lift that shifts the center of mass away from the back leg/rubber, creating more range of motion for the back leg's active drive (hip extension, knee extension, hip abduction) once it begins.
- [02:04](https://youtu.be/NHt_fvKcXJ8?t=124) Studying Fernando Rodney's slide-step delivery, Brewster noticed Rodney leaned his weight toward the target before driving off the back leg; using that pattern he got up to touching the mid-90s before an elbow injury.
- [03:05](https://youtu.be/NHt_fvKcXJ8?t=185) After the injury he experimented with a Tim Lincecum-style aggressive leg lift; his very first attempt, even out of sync, produced a 3 mph jump in average velocity over his slide-step mechanics, and the gain was consistent once he was healthy again.
- [09:10](https://youtu.be/NHt_fvKcXJ8?t=550) Two drift styles: "coil and drift" (Aroldis Chapman links the forward shift to a counter-rotation) versus "straight on target and drift" (Jacob deGrom shifts forward with no coil, moving dynamically through his balance point).
- [10:05](https://youtu.be/NHt_fvKcXJ8?t=605) Counterexamples: Marcus Stroman and Nathan Eovaldi throw hard without an aggressive weight shift, staying back through leg lift and generating momentum by sitting deep into the back leg instead.
- [10:51](https://youtu.be/NHt_fvKcXJ8?t=651) One Tread athlete went from low 90s to mid 90s without touching his weight shift; adding an aggressive drift afterward disrupted his sequencing and slightly decreased velocity, so they reverted to his original pattern.
- [11:28](https://youtu.be/NHt_fvKcXJ8?t=688) Three teaching cues given: lean into the leg lift; shift the balance point 4-6 inches (or rotate the body about 15 degrees) toward the target; stay inside the back foot during leg lift.
- [14:16](https://youtu.be/NHt_fvKcXJ8?t=856) Drill: the step-back windup, coming set, stepping back, then hitting the leg lift and throwing, meant to avoid the excess backward lean that a normal walking windup produces.
- [15:16](https://youtu.be/NHt_fvKcXJ8?t=916) Brewster calls the drift "icing on the cake," only for pitchers who have already fixed hip-shoulder segmentation, arm timing, and front-leg bracing; he does not claim balance-point pitchers are wrong.

## Cues
- **"Lean into your leg lift"** - fixes losing touch with the back leg when athletes are simply told to "drift"; for pitchers who already have a repeatable delivery.
- **"Come to your balance point 4 to 6 inches away from the rubber"** - fixes the lack of forward weight shift while keeping the athlete's existing leg lift feel; for both coil-style and straight-on-target-style deliveries.
- **"Stay inside your back foot"** (during leg lift) - fixes falling through the leg lift and losing contact with the back leg; for pitchers learning the drift via the step-back drill.

## Anti-cues
- none

## Drills
- **Step-back windup** - come set in the normal delivery, step back, then hit the leg lift and throw; builds a repeatable forward weight shift and staying inside the back foot; for pitchers learning the drift who feel flat-footed or lean too far back in a standard walking windup.

## Evidence cited
- Ben Brewster (author, former pro pitcher): first attempt at an aggressive Lincecum-style weight shift produced a 3 mph jump in average velocity over his slide-step mechanics; gain held after returning from injury.
- Unnamed Tread athlete: went from low 90s to mid 90s without changing weight shift; adding the drift afterward caused a slight velocity decrease, so Tread reverted him to his prior pattern.

## Open questions
- The source claims the drift is what "the hardest throwers in the world" tend to do, but names Stroman and Eovaldi as high-velocity counterexamples without reconciling which trait predicts who benefits.
- Brewster states Tread has "not completely figured out how to do this in a repeatable manner with everybody" and attributes success partly to athleticism, without giving a method to screen for it beforehand.
