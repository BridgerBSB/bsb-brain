# Overtraining: A Serious Problem

## TL;DR
- Overtraining is training without respecting recovery; novices get stronger from recovering, not from the workout itself, so hard two-a-day conditioning without rest days causes injury, not toughness.
- Recommends Rippetoe's Starting Strength linear progression (every other day, adding weight each session on Back Squat, Press, Bench Press, Deadlift, Power Clean) as the correct novice model, moving to weekly-planned intermediate programming once recovery can't keep up.
- Draws the line between soreness and injury: if range of motion stays compromised and pain persists, that's an injury, and the fix offered for an overtrained/underfed athlete is up to 6000 calories a day, not more conditioning.

## Claims
- **Overtraining: A Serious Problem** - High school conditioning is often run twice a day (5 AM and 6 PM sessions), including a 2-mile "warm up" run, 100m sprints, and plyometric work; vomiting is treated as proof the workout and coaching are hard enough.
- **Overtraining: A Serious Problem** - Novices do not get stronger when they train, they get stronger when they recover; overloading disrupts homeostasis on purpose, overtraining disrupts it without allowing recovery.
- **Overtraining: A Serious Problem** - Cites Mark Rippetoe's Starting Strength as a good novice model: the lifter trains every other day and adds weight each session on Back Squat, Press, Bench Press, Deadlift, and Power Clean.
- **Training for Novices (aka High School Athletes)** - Lays out a three-phase cycle: load phase (disrupts homeostasis), recovery phase, and supercompensation phase (the body adapts and gets stronger).
- **Dangers of Overtraining** - Constant training without a recovery cycle still produces gains in strict novices, but at a much slower rate due to overtraining and stress.
- **Personal Stories of High School Athletes** - A previously coached athlete reported weightlifting and sprint workouts with less than 16 hours of rest between some sessions, and remained sore despite a 20-minute recovery jog, 10 minutes of leg elevation, 3 hours of icing, icy hot, and foam rolling.
- **Personal Stories of High School Athletes** - Advises the athlete to sleep, foam roll, take contrast baths (a full ice bath followed immediately by a hot shower), and eat as much as 6000 calories per day, including pizza and McDonald's daily.
- **Overtraining Has to Stop** - When range of motion stays compromised and pain does not resolve, that is not normal soreness, it is an injury caused by a poorly designed program.
- **Overtraining Has to Stop** - Defines intermediate programming as planning a week of workouts in advance rather than increasing load every session, and states most athletes never progress past this stage and can still gain from it for years.

## Evidence cited
- **Training for Novices (aka High School Athletes)** - Cites Mark Rippetoe's Starting Strength linear-progression program (every-other-day training, adding weight each session on five core lifts) as the model of correct novice programming.
- **Personal Stories of High School Athletes** - An anonymous former athlete's training log (back-to-back weightlifting/sprint days, under 16 hrs rest) used as a real-world case of overtraining symptoms.

## Open questions
- The author asserts "most athletes will never progress past" intermediate programming and can gain from it "for years" without presenting supporting data.
- The recommendation to eat up to 6000 calories/day of pizza and McDonald's for an overtrained/underfed athlete is given without nutritional evidence or a stated population (age, weight, training load).
