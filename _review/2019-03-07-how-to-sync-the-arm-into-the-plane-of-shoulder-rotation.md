# How to Sync the Arm into the Plane of Shoulder Rotation

## TL;DR
- Max velocity needs the arm to enter and stay in the plane of shoulder rotation; elbow should be in line with the shoulders (90 degrees of shoulder abduction) at foot strike, all the way through release.
- Two failure patterns: a low elbow/arm drag at the start of rotation, and forward trunk flexion or an early-exiting arm at the finish, often from trying too hard to throw strikes or "get behind the ball."
- Fixes are constraint- and cue-based (patient torso rotation, long toss variants, diagnostic drills); one case example shows a gain from upper 80s/low 90s to 94-96 mph after adding rear-leg drive and shoulder tilt.

## Claims
- What about the arm? - shoulder abduction at landing should be 90 degrees, elbow in line with the shoulders, for the arm to sync into the plane of shoulder rotation.
- Low Elbow / Arm Dragging - two athletes with this fault were throwing 3-5 mph below their predicted velocity.
- Flexion based / linear finish - forward trunk flexion at release, often an attempt to "throw strikes" or "get behind the ball," pushes the arm down to the glove-side hip; this is a bigger velocity killer than the arm exiting the plane late while the torso keeps rotating.
- So how do we fix it? / #1 - recommends tracking quality pitches above 85-90% of max velocity instead of only strike percentage, to keep pitchers in attack mode.
- #2: Long toss - long tossing on an arc can worsen a low elbow issue; low elbow athletes should long toss on a line instead.
- #3: Patient torso rotation - let the arm spiral to the top of the "staircase" before torso rotation begins, so the drive happens from the first instant of rotation.
- #4: Find the disconnect - crow hop long toss, blended mound throws with mound plyos, and intent bullpens with stand-in hitters starting at 57-58 feet, used to isolate where the sync breaks down.
- Case example - Andrew Owen went from upper 80s/low 90s to 94-96 mph after adding rear-leg drive ("flatbed") and shoulder tilt ("ferris wheel") to a purely transverse-plane delivery, and was signed by the Cardinals.
- Comparison - Brandon Morrow's 97 mph fastball is shown as a delivery matching a featured HS pitcher's mechanics, minus the rotational flaw at ball release.

## Cues
- **"let the arm spiral its way up before beginning to yank the torso through"** - fixes low elbow/arm drag at the start of rotation
- **"wait until the arm gets to the top of the staircase and drive down through the hallway"** - fixes low elbow/arm drag; staircase analogy for patient torso rotation
- **"throw through the center of the catcher"** - fixes steering/aiming at a small target; for pitchers who forward-flex or ease off intent to be precise

## Anti-cues
- **"get behind the ball"** - taught to fix wildness/throwing strikes; actually causes forward trunk flexion and a pushy, linear finish that takes the arm out of the plane of rotation; per Ben Brewster

## Drills
- **Long toss (on a line)** - low elbow athletes long toss driving the ball down and on a line rather than on an arc; builds keeping the arm in plane while still getting velocity feedback; for low elbow/arm-drag pitchers
- **Intent bullpens with stand-in hitters** - starting at 57-58 feet and working back to full distance; builds blending high-intensity rotational finish with in-game command

## Evidence cited
- Andrew Owen: velocity bump from upper 80s/low 90s to 94-96 mph, signed by the Cardinals, attributed to added rear-leg drive and shoulder tilt.
- Two unnamed athletes with low elbow/arm drag throwing 3-5 mph below their predicted velocity given size and strength numbers.

## Open questions
- The source states forward trunk flexion at release is a bigger velocity killer than an arm that exits the plane late while the torso keeps rotating, but gives no numbers comparing the two.
- No cure-all is claimed for the flexion/pushy finish; the article lists things to avoid rather than a fix, which leaves the actual correction underspecified.
