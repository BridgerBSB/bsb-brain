# Customized Mechanics: Lateral Power

## TL;DR
- Lateral jump distance (a single-leg broad jump to the opposite side) predicted college pitchers' throwing velocity better than ten other common power and speed tests, per the author's own study of 40+ athletes.
- The key threshold: a pitcher who jumps beyond 74 inches but throws under 80 mph has a leak somewhere up the chain, not a lack of raw lower-body power.
- Body weight matters almost as much as jump distance for predicting velocity, and a left/right jump discrepancy over 20% is treated as an injury red flag.

## Claims
- Lateral Power: lateral jump distance outperformed 60-yard dash, 10-yard dash, med ball squat & throw, med ball scoop & throw, vertical jump, single-leg vertical jump (R&L), hop & stop, broad jump, triple broad jump, and ten-yard hop test at predicting throwing velocity, in a study of 40+ college pitchers.
- What's the Test?: stand on the back leg (right for a right-hander), jump laterally as far as possible, land on both feet within a couple of inches of each other, measure the distance.
- How to Use This Information?: a pitcher who jumps beyond 74 inches but throws under 80 mph has power that isn't transferring up the chain; MacWilliams et al. (1998) found back-leg force correlated with velocity overall but identified cases where more force produced slower velocity.
- Add POWER?: body weight was nearly as strong a predictor of throwing velocity as jump distance; the author adapted a broad-jump power formula (from conversation with Dr. Bryan Mann) to combine mass and distance into one score.
- It's All in the Hips!!: borrowing from Kevin Neeld, three confounders limit raw lateral jump distance: limb length, flexibility, and hip/pelvic structure.
- The Neeld Hip Factor Test: measures the widest stance a player can hold with hands off the ground; the author treats this as an estimate of max usable stride length.
- Bonus: Injury Screening!!: a left/right lateral jump discrepancy greater than 20% is flagged as a potential injury risk.

## Evidence cited
- Author's own thesis/study of 40+ college pitchers correlating lateral jump distance to throwing velocity.
- MacWilliams et al. (1998): back-leg force correlated with velocity, but some athletes produced more force with slower velocity.
- Carter Capps cited as an athlete whose mechanics let him handle unusually high force production (high-90s velocity).

## Open questions
- The MacWilliams finding (more back-leg force sometimes means slower velocity) sits uneasily with using lateral jump distance as a velocity KPI; the article resolves it only by invoking "technical mastery" without further data.
- The stride-length application of the Neeld Hip Factor Test is presented as a logical extension, not something validated against actual velocity or injury outcomes in this piece.
