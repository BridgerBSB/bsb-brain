# Why Have I Lost Fastball Velocity?

## TL;DR
- In-season velocity loss, with no mechanical change and no injury, is attributed to not working hard enough in-season.
- The prescription is throwing a baseball up to six days per week in-season, building volume gradually as the pitcher tolerates it.
- The argument rests on one example (Felix Hernandez's long toss) and physiology asserted without citation, so treat it as a stated position, not evidence.

## Claims
- Opening: A pitcher losing velocity over a season with no mechanical change and no apparent injury is told "You don't work hard enough in-season."
- Felix Hernandez quote: Hernandez long tosses every day, at roughly 280 to 300 feet, with a lot of height. Most longer-tossing pitchers go 200 to 225 feet. The quoted source says this builds strength and extension.
- Throwing volume: Reaching a velocity ceiling requires throwing "a LOT of fastballs." The author says coaches overlook this while trying to protect the arm.
- Rest advice criticised: Advice not to lift the day after pitching, or to throw less in-season to "conserve the bullets," is called "exactly backwards." The author says it leads to more arm injuries and lower velocity or effectiveness.
- Physiology: Humans adapt to many types and frequencies of stress, and the author says the "rest" camp misreads this.
- Frequency target: Throw more often and with more volume as tolerated, ideally six days per week including in-season.
- Throwing vs pitching: More frequent throwing conditions the arm's structures, lets the pitcher feel mechanical changes, and aids control.
- Non-competitive work: A pitcher must learn to throw before pitching, and that needs non-competitive throwing, bullpens included.
- Comments: A reader asks which day to skip when building to six days per week. The reply says skipping the day after an 80 to 100 pitch start is fine, and the author likes light batting practice then.
