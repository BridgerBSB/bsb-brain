# How Do Weighted Baseballs Work? Total vs. Peak Force | Driveline Baseball

## TL;DR
- Overload balls (heavier, e.g. 7 oz) raise total force on the arm but lower peak force at release; underload balls (lighter, e.g. 3 oz) do the reverse, raising peak force while lowering total force.
- Argues overload work should dominate a program first because the body adapts to chronic total-force stress; peak-force (underload) work only expresses that adaptation and is risky without an overload base first.
- Cites the 2014 College World Series champion Vanderbilt as a program user and mentions 2 oz balls reportedly thrown over 113 mph, without giving the study behind either number.

## Claims
- [00:01](https://youtu.be/KzcZmQkKFmk?t=1) Driveline calls itself the "foremost authority" on weighted-ball and plyocare training, citing unspecified peer-reviewed research and internal lab testing, and states the 2014 College World Series champion Vanderbilt used its programs.
- [01:02](https://youtu.be/KzcZmQkKFmk?t=62) A 5 oz ball thrown at 90 mph carries an inertial mass of roughly 20 to 40 lbs at maximum external rotation (forearm layback); moving to a 7 oz ball (2 oz overload) raises stress at that loaded position, called total force, while peak force at release is lower because the heavier ball is harder to accelerate.
- [02:03](https://youtu.be/KzcZmQkKFmk?t=123) Total force rises and peak force falls with overload implements; the reverse happens with underload implements (a 3 oz ball, 2 oz underload): less stress in the layback position but faster acceleration, so peak force rises while total force falls.
- [03:05](https://youtu.be/KzcZmQkKFmk?t=185) Overload training is described as safer because the body's adaptation is built for chronic total-force stress ("fitness" in the strict sense: adaptation and motor unit recruitment), not sudden peak-force exposure.
- [04:08](https://youtu.be/KzcZmQkKFmk?t=248) Peak-force stress the body has not adapted to is framed as high risk (a gunshot wound used as an extreme illustration); throwing underload balls without an overload base first is called a "serious risk."
- [04:08](https://youtu.be/KzcZmQkKFmk?t=248) Hold-based arm care programs (named: NPA, "Hold In Velocity Plus") are described as intending to train the posterior shoulder to accept more force, but the source says holds "don't necessarily get the job done," citing its own blog, and states it no longer uses holds.
- [05:08](https://youtu.be/KzcZmQkKFmk?t=308) Overload training is framed as building "the base," "the engine," and "the transmission" (total-force capacity); peak-force training only expresses that underlying capacity, and training peak force without a total-force base is called inefficient.
- [06:09](https://youtu.be/KzcZmQkKFmk?t=369) Compliance with overload work "for a long period" is presented as the prerequisite before peak-force work; pitchers seen throwing 103 mph or 2 oz balls over 113 mph reportedly built that overload base first.
