# The Dreaded Radar Gun

## TL;DR
- Pulling out the radar gun mid-lesson makes a pitcher tense up and muscle the ball, so the mechanical work is lost.
- Use the gun to set an entry velocity and log it with other measures, not to chase a number every bullpen.
- Judge the trend line over time: a 71 mph pitcher may sit anywhere from 65 to 77 mph depending on the day.

## Claims
- Opening scenario: a coach introduces a mechanical focus, then brings out the gun; the pitcher tenses, muscles the ball, and loses the focus. Velocity drops and the lesson is wasted.
- Injury paragraph: using the gun on younger, highly competitive pitchers can cause injury, because it gets them to throw as hard as possible on a mound with stress their arm may not be ready for.
- Program use: Driveline uses the gun in bullpens, but only to record entry velocities as a starting point, with the expectation of improvement later.
- Program use: a pitcher should not try to throw 90 mph while sitting 78 mph, just as a lifter would not squat 300 pounds at a 200 pound capacity. Starting low is fine.
- Program use: the gun belongs in a training log next to strength (1RM squat and deadlift), speed (60 yard dash, 20 yard shuttle), power (vertical leap, broad jump), and sport-specific measures (fastball velocity, bat speed).
- Trend line: do not bring the gun to every bullpen and expect a big reading. Gains come from a good workout program, and only over time.
- Trend line: session-to-session spread is large. A pitcher who comes in at 71 mph may be as low as 65 on bad days and as high as 77 on others.
- Trend line: if the best-fit line is going up, something is going right, so do not sweat individual sessions.

## Open questions
- The injury claim for young, competitive pitchers is asserted with no data or study.
- The 65 to 77 mph day-to-day range is a hypothetical example, not a measured spread. Other Driveline sources that use velocity as a session-level readout may conflict with the "ignore single sessions" advice.
