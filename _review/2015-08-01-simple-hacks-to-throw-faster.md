# Simple Hacks to Throw Faster

## TL;DR
- Author argues small/skinny hard throwers (using Fernando Rodney as the case study) throw hard through efficient kinetic-chain sequencing, not just size or genetics.
- Three mechanical tweaks he copied from Rodney: stay closed longer with more shoulder counter-rotation, drive harder off the back leg directly at the target, and finish with a more violent pull-down rather than spinning through release.
- Self-reported result: went from an 88-90 mph pitcher (touching 91) to 90-94 mph (touching 95) after a summer of drilling these tweaks.

## Claims
- [Small flamethrowers have the most efficient mechanics] Harder throwers generally have more efficient mechanics, meaning less energy leakage transferring force through the legs, hips, torso, shoulder, and elbow into the ball; small/skinny pitchers have no size advantage so they need this efficiency more than bigger throwers.
- [Fernando Rodney, listed generously at 5'11," touches 100 mph from a slide-step.] Rodney, listed at 5'11", touches 100 mph throwing exclusively from a slide-step out of a side-arm slot.
- [#1: Fernando Rodney stays back and closed better than other pitchers.] Rodney holds his front hip closed and internally rotated until the last moment before landing, keeps his upper body stacked over his back hip, and counter-rotates his shoulders (showing his numbers to the batter) to build additional torque before front-foot landing.
- [#1: Fernando Rodney stays back and closed better than other pitchers.] Other hard throwers cited as sharing this stay-closed/counter-rotation pattern: Bob Feller, Aroldis Chapman (105 mph), and Lance McCullers Jr. (100 mph, in high school).
- [#2: Rodney drives insanely hard off his back leg] Rodney drives off his back leg on a line directly at his target (horizontal, not vertical), building linear energy early that gets captured as rotational energy once his front foot lands; the author calls the resulting lower-half/upper-half disconnect "linear distraction" and ties it to Trevor Bauer's discussion of the same concept.
- [#3: His finish is more violent and explosive.] Rodney does not spin through release; he pulls down with force, propelling his upper body well over his lead leg at release.
- [The results?] After a summer drilling the stay-closed and back-leg-drive tweaks, the author's slide-step long toss (no crow-hop) went from a 315-330 foot range to 350-380 feet on his best day, and that fall he touched 93 mph on flat ground for the first time.
- [The results?] Using a crow-hop, he hit 98, 100, and 102 mph in three throws; that following spring he hit 95 mph for the first time, in-game, on a cold February day.

## Evidence cited
- Author's own self-tracked long toss distances (315-330 ft to 350-380 ft) and velocity progression (88-90 touching 91, to 90-94 touching 95), self-reported, no measurement method given beyond "long toss" and "flat ground"/mound readings.
- Named athletes with velocity numbers: Aroldis Chapman (105 mph), Lance McCullers Jr. (100 mph in high school), Fernando Rodney (100 mph, 5'11", from a slide-step).

## Open questions
- The author explicitly separates "efficient" (best for force production) from "optimal" (best for durability/injury prevention), and says Rodney's mechanics are the former, not necessarily the latter; the article does not resolve whether the same tweaks carry injury risk.
- All velocity and distance gains are one athlete's self-experimentation over a single summer with no control condition, so the tweaks-caused-the-gain claim is asserted, not tested.
