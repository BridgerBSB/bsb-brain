# How Does Nate Pearson Throw 104 MPH? A Breakdown.

## TL;DR
- Ben Brewster breaks down Pearson's mechanics: stacked posture, a held rotational hinge, a late pelvis unload, and a compact arm action for a tall thrower.
- Intent is only useful when it is timed: the throw builds from the lower half and the arm adds effort late. Trunk extension is a byproduct of that sequencing, not something to reach for.
- Pearson went from 94 mph into college to 102 leaving it, and the source says frame and genetics still carry much of the 104.

## Claims
- [01:02](https://youtu.be/wi6H5WdLjUY?t=62) During the weight shift he stays stacked and centered over the pelvis and heel, not tipping forward or leaking onto the toes, then unloads downhill into landing.
- [03:04](https://youtu.be/wi6H5WdLjUY?t=184) He does not use a vertical shin. His drive position is internal-rotation dominant, which requires good hip internal rotation; hard throwers exist in both categories.
- [04:05](https://youtu.be/wi6H5WdLjUY?t=245) Which shin angle suits an athlete is dictated by hip anatomy; if unsure, split test both for velocity, command and feel.
- [05:06](https://youtu.be/wi6H5WdLjUY?t=306) He holds the pelvis coiled over the femur through the linear move, belt buckle still closed, then unloads at about foot landing. Opening at leg lift is "swinging the gate".
- [06:06](https://youtu.be/wi6H5WdLjUY?t=366) The pelvis is roughly 45 degrees open at landing and rotates down, matching the plane of the shoulders.
- [07:07](https://youtu.be/wi6H5WdLjUY?t=427) Very tall throwers often benefit from a shorter arm path, elbow nearer 90 degrees, which helps repeat the release point. Do not tense up and lose scap retraction.
- [11:15](https://youtu.be/wi6H5WdLjUY?t=675) At 100+ mph most throwers are throwing at true max intent (he and Nolan Ryan both grunt), but the arm stays relaxed until the pec and lat stretch.
- [12:15](https://youtu.be/wi6H5WdLjUY?t=735) "Throw with intent" can help one athlete and ruin another's sequencing by bringing the arm in too soon.
- [13:17](https://youtu.be/wi6H5WdLjUY?t=797) Extension out front is a byproduct of a well-sequenced unload; bucket and towel drills fake it.
- [17:20](https://youtu.be/wi6H5WdLjUY?t=1040) Same mechanics on a less powerful athlete might top out near 92. Mechanics are one variable among frame, power and mobility.

## Cues
- **"staying stacked, staying centered"** - fixes tipping the torso forward or leaking onto the toes in the weight shift; for pitchers generally
- **"allowing the throw to build its way up the chain"** - fixes the arm taking over the throw too soon; for pitchers who muscle up
- **"relaxing and floating the arm up the staircase"** - fixes tensing the arm and cutting off scap retraction while compacting; for taller pitchers with long levers

## Anti-cues
- **"get on top of the ball" / "get out front of the ball"** - taught to fix lack of extension at release; actually causes the athlete to yank the front side open and reach with the arm; per Ben Brewster, Tread

## Evidence cited
- Nate Pearson: up to 104 mph, 6'6", about 240 lb; 94 mph entering college, 102 leaving.
- Joe Kelly, named as a pitcher who moved from a long arm spiral to a more compact one.
- Nolan Ryan, named as Pearson's idol, with a similar elbow angle and intent.
- Archimedes "commonero" (sic), shown as another long-armed thrower with large extension.

## Open questions
- Brewster's hypothesis that long arms explain extension at release is stated without data.
- The internal-rotation drive position contradicts the common vertical-shin cue; the source's answer is to split test per athlete.
