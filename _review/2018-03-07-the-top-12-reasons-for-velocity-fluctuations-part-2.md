# The Top 12 Reasons For Velocity Fluctuations (part 2)

## TL;DR
- In-season velocity swings are usually driven by non-mechanical factors: time of day, arousal level, sleep and travel, temporary post-throwing mobility loss, and inconsistent focus, not by changing mechanics.
- Argues pitchers should not tinker with mechanics in-season; instead build repeatability through pre-game, pre-outing, and pre-pitch routines.
- Author's own velocity dropped from 93-94 mph to 88-92 mph in his first pro outing after a focus lapse, then returned to normal within two days once he refocused on attacking hitters.

## Claims
- **Time of Day** - a cited study suggests trying to peak within the first 4 hours after waking may significantly reduce performance; velocity can be down a couple ticks for early morning games or bullpens.
- **Physiological arousal / stimulants** - performance follows a bell-curve relationship with arousal; both too little and too much reduce output.
- **Physiological arousal / stimulants** - author avoided stimulants in high-crowd game environments (5,000 fans) because added arousal pushed him over the edge; shaking hands signal excess arousal.
- **Sleep quality / travel** - poor sleep, worsened by sleep aids or alcohol, reduces high-quality REM sleep and correlates with lower velocity; describes pro travel of finishing a game then a 12-hour overnight trip with only a couple hours of sleep before the next game.
- **Fluctuations in mobility** - post-throwing muscle shortening causes temporary loss of shoulder internal rotation, forearm supination, shoulder flexion, and horizontal abduction, one reason velocity tends to drop on day two or three of consecutive outings; a lefty pitcher is shown with more than a 45 degree side-to-side difference in elbow supination.
- **Fluctuations in mobility** - Brandon Morrow threw 94 mph instead of his usual 100 mph by his 7th outing across 7 World Series games, attributed to insufficient recovery.
- **Inconsistent throwing focus** - author sat 93-94 mph at his Super Regional, then was 88-92 mph in his first pro outing despite adequate rest; refocusing on attacking hitters rather than placing pitches tentatively restored his velocity within 2 days, and he was promoted from Rookie ball the next day.
- **Mechanical repeatability** - if landing spot, release point, or hip-shoulder separation vary pitch to pitch, velocity will be affected; states that working on mechanics is the wrong fix once in-season.
- **Mechanical repeatability** - team devoted 5 to 10 minutes most practices to dry, ball-less reps of the pre-pitch routine (get sign, come set, breathe, repeat a personal keyword, deliver), done on the road Thursday nights on the opposing team's mound.
- **Conclusion** - frames the whole piece around not abandoning a training program over a 2 mph dip; recommends making a logical adjustment and refocusing instead.

## Drills
- **Pre-pitch routine rehearsal** - dry reps with no ball, rehearsing sign, set, breath, and a personal keyword before delivery, run 5-10 minutes most practices; builds mechanical repeatability through a consistent pre-pitch state; for pitchers maintaining velocity consistency in-season.

## Evidence cited
- Cited study (linked, no effect size given) on peaking within 4 hours of waking reducing performance.
- Author's own velocity log: 93-94 mph pre-pro, 88-92 mph in first pro outing, back to normal in 2 days, followed by promotion from Rookie ball.
- Brandon Morrow: 94 mph vs. a typical 100 mph by his 7th outing across 7 World Series games.
- Lefty pitcher case: more than 45 degree side-to-side difference in elbow supination.

## Open questions
- Claims in-season mechanical work is "the last thing you need to do" to fix repeatability, contradicting common practice of mechanical tinkering, based on personal experience rather than data.
- The time-of-day claim rests on one cited study without stating its effect size or athlete population.
