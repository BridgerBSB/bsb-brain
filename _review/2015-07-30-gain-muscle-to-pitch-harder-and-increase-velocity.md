# Gain Muscle to Pitch Harder and Increase Velocity

## TL;DR
- Muscle mass and strength, not mechanics alone, are the biggest limiting factor for velocity in most amateur pitchers, since muscle size is directly proportional to max contractile strength.
- Lean body mass jumps sharply from high school to college to professional levels, a gap too large to be explained by height alone.
- Guideline: build lean body mass toward the top of what your frame can hold; example given is ~205 lbs at 12% body fat as a minimum target for a 6'3" pitcher.

## Claims
- "Why building muscle mass will help your pitching velocity": muscle size is directly proportional to maximum contractile strength; velocity comes from generating and transferring force efficiently through the lower body, trunk, shoulder, elbow, wrist to the ball, and most amateur pitchers, especially high school and below, simply aren't very strong or powerful.
- "I'm about to blow your mind.": lean body mass rises sharply from high school to college to professional baseball, per the LBM-by-level chart; the author states the gap is disproportionately large relative to height differences between levels, "strongly suggesting" muscle mass drives it.
- "I'm about to blow your mind.": bigger pitchers don't always throw hard and small pitchers sometimes throw hard, though rare, and "small" for a big leaguer is still about 170-180 lbs.
- "Exploiting this information to throw gas": author's own example, at 6'3" and ~215 lbs at 12% body fat, is above his stated minimum target (~205 lbs at 12% for that height), but the guideline tables suggest as much as 12 lbs more lean mass could still be gained.
- "You have what it takes": high school or college pitchers more than 30 lbs below the low end of the guideline chart "have some work to do"; the recommendation is to get as big and strong as the frame allows while maintaining movement quality, and to target the general MLB weight range for one's height.
- "References": cites 7 peer-reviewed studies on anthropometrics and strength-velocity relationships in professional and college baseball players (Hoffman et al. 2009; Lehman, Drinkwater & Behm 2013; Mangine et al. 2013 x2; Nuttgen 1976; Spaniol 2003; Young, Stokes, Walker & Newham 1981).

## Open questions
- The claim that the LBM jump between levels is "in large part" due to muscle mass, rather than height or other factors, is asserted from the chart but not directly tied to a specific cited study isolating that cause.
- The lean body mass guideline numbers by height are shown only in an image/chart not reproduced in the transcript text, so besides the author's own example (~205 lbs at 12% body fat for 6'3"), the specific targets aren't independently verifiable from the raw source.
