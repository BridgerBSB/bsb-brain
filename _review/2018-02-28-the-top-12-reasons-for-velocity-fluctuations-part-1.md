# The Top 12 Reasons For Velocity Fluctuations (part 1)

## TL;DR
- Frames outings into three buckets: A game (~10% of outings, lights out), B game (~80%, decent), C game (~10%, flat), and argues most day-to-day velocity dips are explainable rather than alarming.
- Covers six factors in this half of the series: throwing workload, in-season lifting load, caloric balance, macronutrient intake, weather, and mound conditions.
- Key numeric guidance: in-season, cut lifting sets in half while keeping about 90% of the off-season load, and eat roughly 1,000 calories during the 4-6 hour game-day window when players typically don't.

## Claims
- **1. Cumulative fatigue from throwing** - poorly managed total throwing workload (count and intensity, including bullpen warmups and practice throws, not just game pitches) drives velocity swings; example given is a reliever throwing about 50 in-game pitches a week but also 90-135 max-effort bullpen warmup throws.
- **2. Cumulative fatigue from lifting** - stopping in-season strength training causes a decline after a few weeks; recommends cutting number of sets in half and keeping about 90% of the regular weight used, with lift timing built around the outing schedule.
- **3. Caloric Balance** - recommends about 1,000 calories during the 4-6 hour daily field window when players often eat nothing, tracking bodyweight for sharp weekly drops.
- **4. Macronutrient breakdown** - targets roughly 1 gram of protein per pound of bodyweight per day; author personally aims for 200-230 grams per day.
- **5. Weather/temperature** - extreme cold, heat, wind, and rain affect day-to-day velocity.
- **6. Mound conditions/specifications** - better traction (clay vs. turf) and taller mounds tend to raise velocity; taller mounds are said to benefit north-south arm slots (Koufax) more than east-west, low-slot pitchers (Randy Johnson), a comparison the author labels anecdotal.
