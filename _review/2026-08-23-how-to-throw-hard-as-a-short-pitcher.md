# How To Throw Hard As A Short Pitcher

## TL;DR
- Tread's internal database (7,000+ athletes) shows the main gap between short (5'11" and under) and tall (6'4"+) 92+ mph throwers is trunk rotational velocity, not mass or lever length.
- Short 92+ mph pitchers run a 190 lb median bodyweight versus 225 lb for tall 92+ throwers (35 lb gap), but past 92 mph, bodyweight stops correlating with velocity band, pointing to mechanical efficiency as the limiter.
- Short pitchers get a built-in vertical approach angle edge from their lower release height; the source recommends leaning into a north-south, high-spin-efficiency arsenal to exploit it.

## Claims
- [13:15] Comparing 5'11"-and-under vs 6'4"-and-above pitchers both throwing 92+, trunk rotational velocity was the biggest measured difference between the groups.
- [16:18] Hard-throwing short pitchers averaged a 45% increase in lead knee extension velocity at max layback and at ball release relative to tall pitchers.
- [19:52]-[20:21] Across 7,000+ athletes, the median weight for pitchers 5'11" or under throwing 92+ was 190 lb, versus 225 lb for the tall 92+ group.
- [21:22] Within the short 92+ group, bodyweight did not correlate with which velocity band (92-93, 93-95, 95+) a pitcher landed in.
- [22:20] Strength benchmarks (vertical jump, broad jump, DB bench, back squat, trap bar deadlift, DB row, 3-5 reps) for the short hard-throwing group were described as solid but not exceptional.
- [23:25] Case: Brian Curley went from 5'10", 175 lb, throwing 68 mph to 212 lb touching 98 mph over four years.
- [29:30] Case: Antoine Kelly added 40+ lb, going from 80 to 98 mph, and could use a slower tempo once mass and levers increased.
- [32:33] Case: Myles Garrett, listed 5'10", 175 lb, hit 101 mph shuffle / 97+ mph flat-ground, attributed to rotational explosiveness and a near-40-inch vertical.
- [37:39] Undersized pitchers show elite vertical approach angle in Tread's data due to lower release height, producing a flatter, higher-perceived-rise fastball despite reduced extension.
- [35:37] The presenter's own progression, 85 mph as a college walk-on to 95+ mph as a senior, came from a "throw hard easy" approach without radar or motion-capture feedback.

## Cues
- **"Throw hard easy"** - fixes over-effort throwing that masks poor sequencing; for pitchers training efficiency on low-to-medium effort days.

## Drills
- **Long toss** - increasing-distance outdoor throwing; builds feel for ball flight, looseness, and free arm action, especially for four-seam pitchers who rely on ride and tilt.
- **Pull-downs** - throw with a running start down a line; builds transfer of linear (running) momentum into the mound delivery, used to diagnose a gap between run-and-gun velo and mound velo.
- **Rotational med ball / cable / Kaiser work** - rotational movements at varying loads and speeds (cable chops, med ball throws, explosive Kaiser rotations); builds explosive hip and trunk rotational velocity, distinct from straight-plane lifts like bench and squat.

## Evidence cited
- Tread internal dataset, 7,000+ athletes trained since 2015, with a subset of "a couple hundred" pitchers reaching 92+ mph at 5'11" or under [20:21].
- Named results: Brian Curley (68 to 98 mph over 4 years) [23:25], Antoine Kelly (80 to 98 mph, +40 lb) [29:30], Myles Garrett (97-101 mph at listed 175 lb) [32:33], Nick Bond (100 mph at listed 160 lb) [25:27], Yoshinobu Yamamoto (5'10", 176 lb, up to 99 mph) [18:18].

## Open questions
- [21:22] The claim that bodyweight decorrelates from velocity band above 92 mph is asserted from internal data with no stated sample size or split methodology for that specific breakdown.
- [38:39] The proposed advantages of a shorter torso and shorter arm-swing pendulum are explicitly framed by the source as untested ("something we want to test... it's plausible"), not established findings.
