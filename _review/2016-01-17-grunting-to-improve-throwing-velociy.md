# Grunting to Improve Throwing Velociy

## TL;DR
- Forceful exhalation ("grunting") stiffens the trunk at the moment force needs to transfer from legs/hips into the arm; a 2014 tennis study found it raised serve and forehand velocity by about 5%.
- The benefit comes from timing, not constant tension: muscles need to contract to stabilize and then relax quickly so the arm can whip through, per the double impulse theory.
- Brewster's own informal test with about 6 hard-throwing teammates found grunted fastballs were consistently their hardest pitches, with a 1-2 mph bump.

## Claims
- **The Effects of "Grunting" on Serve and Forehand Velocities in Collegiate Tennis Players - O'Connell et al. (2014):** grunting increased serve and forehand velocity by about 5% in NCAA tennis players, including some who did not normally grunt.
- **What is a grunt?:** a grunt is a forceful expiration of air; the "kiai" forceful exhale used in martial arts increased handgrip force by 7% (Welch and Tschampl). The alternative bracing method, the Valsalva maneuver (forceful inhalation), raises blood pressure and can cause blackout, making exhalation-based grunting the safer option.
- **How does grunting help?:** per the study authors, forceful expiration recruits more thoracic trunk musculature through a central-command feedforward pathway, increasing force output from the pectoralis major and external obliques.
- **How exactly does grunting help increase velocity?:** a more stable trunk lets other muscles produce more force both dynamically and statically; abdominal muscles both accelerate and stabilize the trunk during the tennis serve (Kovacs et al. 2008).
- **Is being stiff a good thing?:** elite athletes can relax muscles up to 8x faster than others (Matveyev, 1981). A contract-then-relax-then-contract pattern ("double impulse theory") shows up in MMA punches and golf swings (McGill, 2014 and 2010). Staying tense through the whole delivery instead of relaxing between contractions will lower velocity.
- **How it works in throwing (in my opinion):** the author's opinion is that the trunk should contract and stiffen only once rotation has built up and is transferring energy to the shoulder and arm, not throughout the whole delivery. Building that rotational speed requires adequate hip internal rotation/abduction and thoracic spine mobility.
- **Practical Application (comments by Ben Brewster):** about 6 teammates tested grunting informally; in every case the 2-3 grunted fastballs mixed into an outing were the hardest pitches thrown. Recommends grunting on every pitch, including off-speed, so the grunt does not tip the pitch type.

## Cues
- **"Do it on every pitch so you don't tip off when you're throwing something off-speed"** - fixes tipping pitch type from an inconsistent effort cue; for pitchers adding a grunt to chase a velocity bump.

## Evidence cited
- O'Connell et al. (2014): NCAA tennis players, forced-grunt condition raised serve/forehand velocity about 5%.
- Welch and Tschampl: martial-arts "kiai" forceful exhale raised handgrip force 7%.
- Kovacs et al. (2008): abdominal muscles accelerate and stabilize the trunk during the serve.
- McGill (2010, 2014): double impulse (contract-relax-contract) pattern in golf swings and MMA punches.
- Matveyev (1981): elite athletes relax muscles up to 8x faster than non-elite.
- Andrew Robb (cited paper, no year given): excessive joint mobility can make it harder to harness power.
- Ben Brewster, informal in-house test: about 6 hard-throwing teammates, grunted fastballs consistently hardest of the outing, roughly 1-2 mph bump.

## Open questions
- No cited study tests grunting on pitching directly; the mechanism is extrapolated from tennis (O'Connell), martial arts (Welch and Tschampl), and golf/MMA (McGill).
- Brewster's 1-2 mph in-house figure comes from about 6 throwers with no control condition, sitting next to the controlled 5% tennis figure as if comparable.
