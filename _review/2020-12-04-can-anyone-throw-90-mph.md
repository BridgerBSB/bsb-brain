# Can Anyone Throw 90 MPH?

## TL;DR
- Reaching 90 mph depends on genetic limits, but training status, mechanics, and playing history move the ceiling more than most people think.
- Brewster lists four predictors of a velocity ceiling: playing history, lever length, mechanical efficiency, and training status.
- His estimate: 55 to 60 percent of high schoolers who touch 82 are theoretically capable of touching 90, though under 1 percent of 80 mph high schoolers actually do.

## Claims
- [00:00](https://youtu.be/EJjTapd9Uro?t=0) People want either "it's all genetic" or "one drill fixes it." Brewster says neither is true. Some people can dunk untrained, some need years, some never will.
- [02:00](https://youtu.be/EJjTapd9Uro?t=120) Projection is more art than science. An athlete can usually perform at least one level above the current one (JV to varsity, varsity to college).
- [03:01](https://youtu.be/EJjTapd9Uro?t=181) Antoine Kelly was 6'5", 165 lb, untrained, mid-upper 80s touching low 90s at 18. Untrained status predicted a big jump.
- [04:02](https://youtu.be/EJjTapd9Uro?t=242) Starting to throw late (16 to 17) lowers the ceiling: a motor learning deficit of tens of thousands of reps, and less humeral retroversion from early throws.
- [05:03](https://youtu.be/EJjTapd9Uro?t=303) Other throwing sports (javelin, quarterback, cricket) carry over very little. Brewster cites the Olympic javelin record holder's Braves tryout.
- [06:04](https://youtu.be/EJjTapd9Uro?t=364) Wingspan may predict lever length better than height. Most MLB pitchers fall between 6'0" and 6'6". Shorter levers may help repeatability, a speculative point.
- [08:08](https://youtu.be/EJjTapd9Uro?t=488) Mechanical efficiency means minimal energy leaks segment to segment. No single "perfect mechanics" exists.
- [09:08](https://youtu.be/EJjTapd9Uro?t=548) Flaw cost: slight is 1 to 2 mph, moderate 2 to 4 mph, severe 5+ mph. Fixing a flaw can take years or never happen.
- [13:13](https://youtu.be/EJjTapd9Uro?t=793) Severe flaws named: less than 150 degrees of layback, rotating as one block, linear follow-through, very soft lead leg, arm very down at landing.
- [14:14](https://youtu.be/EJjTapd9Uro?t=854) Training status: novice has 5 to 7+ mph left, intermediate 1 to 5 mph, advanced 1 to 3 mph.

## Evidence cited
- Antoine Kelly: 18 and untrained, mid-upper 80s; within a year and a half 96 to 98 mph, second-round pick.
- Athlete B (21, intermediate, major timing and follow-through flaws, 87 to 89 mph): now 93 to 95, touching 96.
- Ben Heller (28, advanced, 93 mph average): aim of 1 to 3 mph to return to pre-elbow-surgery level.
- Brewster himself: 73 mph at 15, later 98+ mph after 10+ years.
- Brewster's own estimates of the share of pitchers able to touch 90 (no data or method given).

## Open questions
- The 55 to 60 percent figure for 82 mph throwers and the per-flaw mph costs are estimates with no dataset or method shown.
- The claim that shorter levers aid efficiency is labeled anecdotal and speculative by the speaker.
