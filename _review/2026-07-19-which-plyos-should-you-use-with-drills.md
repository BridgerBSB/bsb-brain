# Which Plyos Should You Use? [With Drills]

## TL;DR
- Heavier plyos (up to 2 lb) lower arm speed and stress, so they're used early to repattern faults like long arm action, early layback, or elbow drag; lighter plyos (5oz-3.5oz) carry more stress and are used once mechanics are cleaner, to speed the arm up.
- Radar testing across plyo weights sorts athletes into types: "muscle-driven" throwers show small velocity jumps between weights (example: 85 to 86 mph), "whippy" throwers show jumps as large as 8-15 mph, which flags which weight they need more work with.
- Recommended dosage: one maximum-intent plyo testing session per week, 3-4 drills tested with the 7oz/5oz/3.5oz balls, across a 3-4 week wave; more frequent max-effort plyo work raises injury risk.

## Claims
- [00:00](https://youtu.be/GQ-v950iAHg?t=0) Most people misuse plyos by throwing max effort without a ramp-up, which causes injury the same way maxing a squat five days straight would.
- [02:02](https://youtu.be/GQ-v950iAHg?t=122) Weight range used: 2 lb, 1 lb, 16oz, 7oz, 5oz, 3.5oz; a baseball is "five and five ounces" (sic); progression should finish closest to baseball weight.
- [03:04](https://youtu.be/GQ-v950iAHg?t=184) Heavier plyos are used early because lower arm speed means less stress; lighter plyos carry more stress because the arm moves faster.
- [04:07](https://youtu.be/GQ-v950iAHg?t=247) Heavy plyo, done in a split stance, isolates the arm and is used to repattern long arm action, early layback, shoulder climb, or elbow drag.
- [07:16](https://youtu.be/GQ-v950iAHg?t=436) "Muscle-driven" throwers show small velocity increments moving from heavier to lighter plyo, e.g. 85 mph with the 7oz to 86 mph with the 5oz.
- [08:18](https://youtu.be/GQ-v950iAHg?t=498) "Whippy" throwers show large velocity jumps by comparison, e.g. 75 mph (7oz) to 83 mph (5oz) to 89-90 mph with a lighter ball, indicating they need more heavy-plyo work for pretension.
- [09:20](https://youtu.be/GQ-v950iAHg?t=560) Pitchers whose elbow protrudes forward into premature extension should avoid heavy plyo, since it drives that pattern; they should stay closer to baseball weight (7oz/5oz).
- [09:20](https://youtu.be/GQ-v950iAHg?t=560) Light plyo (5oz, 3.5oz) is for throwers who already move efficiently and just need to speed the arm up.
- [11:28](https://youtu.be/GQ-v950iAHg?t=688) Max-intent plyo testing is recommended once a week at most, using 3-4 drills tested across a 3-4 week wave.
- [13:32](https://youtu.be/GQ-v950iAHg?t=812) Squeezing the ball too hard dissipates force into the ball's squish, which both lowers the recorded velocity and increases forearm stress.

## Cues
- **"Keep the arm loose"** - fixes tensing up/muscling the throw during repatterning; for pitchers doing heavy plyo drills
- **"Let the arm go"** - fixes forcing the throw before reaching the flip-up/layback position; for pitchers working half-layback feel drills

## Drills
- **Tento's (split-stance heavy plyo)** - split stance, minimal lower-half movement, heavy plyo up to 2 lb; builds arm-path awareness in isolation; for pitchers with long arm action, early layback, shoulder climb, or elbow drag
- **Pivot pick** - heavy plyo thrown off a pivot with lower half removed from the throw; builds isolated arm-path awareness; same population as tento's
- **Half-layback feel drills (self-toss, dangle, dead man)** - arm relaxes into a flip-up position before release; builds the feel of the pelvis leading and the arm unraveling passively; for pitchers who muscle the arm as the main driver
- **Figure eight** - plyo swung in a figure-eight pattern, feeling the weight both directions; builds a loose-arm feel and arm-path awareness; for repatterning arm action
- **One-leg-off-ground throw** - light plyo (5oz/3.5oz) thrown with one leg off the ground; builds a faster, more compact arm path; for efficient throwers increasing arm speed
- **Drop step / quick pick throws** - athletic, low-thought throws off a drop step or quick pick with light plyo; builds speed and tightness under reduced decision time; for efficient throwers increasing arm speed

## Open questions
- The source claims lighter plyos are more stressful than heavier ones, reasoning by analogy to sled sprinting rather than citing a study; this runs against the common assumption that heavier implements are the riskier ones.
- The muscle-driven vs. whippy velocity jumps are given as illustrative examples ("let's say"), not measured results from named athletes, so their precision is unclear.
