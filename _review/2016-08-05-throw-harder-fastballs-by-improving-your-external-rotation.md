# Throw Harder Fastballs By Improving Your External Rotation

## TL;DR
- Pro pitchers show 160-185+ degrees of external rotation (ER) at layback; more arc means more distance to apply force to the ball, which raises velocity.
- ER is trainable through four levers: t-spine mobility, releasing tight anterior shoulder tissue, addressing posterior-side restrictions, and reprogramming arm path with overload/underload implements.
- ER is only one input: arm length, hip-shoulder separation, horizontal abduction, and raw strength can offset a shorter arc, and going past roughly 185 degrees isn't necessarily better and may raise injury risk.

## Claims
- Why is external rotation important - Pro pitchers range from 160 to 185+ degrees of ER; per Seroyer et al. (2010), extreme ER plus forward trunk flexion increases the distance over which force is applied to the ball, generating top velocity.
- Why is external rotation important - Internal rotation following layback can reach upwards of 7,500 degrees per second.
- Why is external rotation important - True layback also requires t-spine extension, t-spine rotation, and scapular posterior tilt; a long throwing history (humeral retroversion) accounts for about 10.6 degrees of added ER in pros who've thrown since childhood.
- Do you need 180+ degrees to throw harder - ER alone doesn't determine velocity; hypermobility beyond the pro range may be detrimental to velocity and potentially dangerous.
- Do you need 180+ degrees to throw harder - Arm length, hip/shoulder separation, and horizontal abduction ("scap loading") can compensate for lower ER, along with raw strength/power.
- Improve thoracic extension/rotation - Assessment: lying on a foam roller with hands clasped behind the head, elbows and head should nearly reach the floor without lumbar extension; seated rotation should reach about 45 degrees.
- Improve posterior tilt/horizontal abduction - Tightness in pec major, pec minor, lats, and subscapularis blocks clean layback; the fix is self-myofascial release followed by full-range pressing and pulling work.
- Eliminate tissue restrictions on the back side - Restrictions in teres minor, infraspinatus, rhomboids, and levator scap can also limit ER, especially with a prior injury history there.
- Re-programming movement - Overload implements (Plyocare balls, wrist weights) drive the arm into layback and remap arm path; underload implements then transfer that new path into higher arm speed. Back off ball weight if the athlete tenses rather than relaxes into layback (the author avoids a 2kg ball for this reason).
- Final Thoughts - Pitchers can expect to gain roughly 5-10 degrees of ER over a season just from throwing.

## Drills
- **Front Rack Reverse Lunge** - anteriorly loaded lunge; builds strength in newly gained t-spine extension range; for pitchers working on layback.
- **Pallof Press (variations)** - anti-rotation press at various angles; builds t-spine rotational control; for pitchers building layback.
- **Pec Barbell Smash** - self-myofascial release on pec major/minor with a barbell; opens new ER range by loosening anterior tissue; for pitchers with tight pecs/lats/subscap.
- **Full Range Dumbbell Bench Press** - pressing through complete range of motion; strengthens newly opened posterior tilt/horizontal abduction range; for pitchers after releasing anterior tightness.
- **Overload Implement Throws** - throwing Plyocare balls or wrist weights heavier than a baseball; builds an efficient arm path and drives the arm into layback; for pitchers with inefficient or pushy arm action.
- **Underload Implement Throws** - throwing lighter-than-normal implements; transfers a newly grooved arm path into higher arm speed and velocity; for pitchers who've already remapped arm path with overload work.

## Evidence cited
- Seroyer et al. (2010), *Sports Health*: extreme ER plus forward trunk flexion generates top velocity.
- Cites humeral retroversion adding about 10.6 degrees of ER in pros with a long throwing history (source study not named beyond in-text mention).
- Lists six additional biomechanics references (Fleisig, Pappas, Dillman, Werner, Feltner, Park) on pitching kinetics/shoulder biomechanics, not individually quoted with numbers.
- References the author's own 2014 Driveline article on overload/underload training as the basis for the implement-constraint claims.

## Open questions
- The strength recommendations (front rack reverse lunge, full-range dumbbell bench) are asserted to add ER but no before/after degree data is given, unlike the pro-range and retroversion numbers cited elsewhere in the piece.
- The claim that hypermobility past roughly 185 degrees may hurt velocity and raise injury risk runs against the common scouting assumption that a looser arm always throws harder; no citation is given for this specific ceiling.
