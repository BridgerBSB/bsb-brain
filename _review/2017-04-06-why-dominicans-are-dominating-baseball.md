# Why Dominicans Are Dominating Baseball

## TL;DR
- Argues the Dominican Republic produces MLB talent at roughly 5 times the US rate while spending far less per athlete, and attributes this to need-based motivation, tool-first development, and imitation-based motor learning in talent hotbeds.
- Criticizes American development culture for overspending on lessons and gear while over-coaching mechanics (balance points, towel drills) instead of athletic movement.
- Flags confounders that complicate the story: PED use, age fabrication, and possibly anthropometric differences, none of which the author has Dominican-specific data for.

## Claims
- ## The Stats - In 2015, 83 of 750 opening day players were Dominican-born (about 11 percent); one Dominican big leaguer exists per roughly 63,000 population versus one American per 307,000, putting DR production at about 5 times the US rate.
- ## The Stats - In the lowest two levels of the White Sox system, 44 percent of players were Dominican-born, rising past 60 percent when counting other foreign-born players.
- ## Money ≠ Advanced development - Average adult salary is about $5,000/year in the DR versus about $40,000 in the US, while a typical American travel-ball kid's annual cost (lessons, fees, one glove/bat) runs over $2,000, meaning the DR produces talent at an estimated 10 to 30 times less spending per athlete.
- #1 They don't want it, they need it - Argues need is a stronger motivator than want, framing Dominican development as driven by economic necessity rather than desire.
- #2 They focus on developing tools over winning games - Claims Dominican prospects are typically signed only if they show plus tools (throwing hard, hitting for power) by age 18, with most signing bonuses in the $50,000 to $100,000 range.
- #3 Talent hotbeds and motor learning through imitation - Names Georgia, Florida, Texas, and Southern California as US hotbeds and credits hotbed advantage partly to motor learning through imitating higher-level patterns.
- #4 Other factors - Notes age fabrication is common and often tolerated by organizations regardless of a prospect's true age.
- #4 Other factors - Cites a skeletal-proportion study: Black athletes have longer legs and shorter trunks than white athletes, Asians the reverse, and Mexican Americans similar proportions to whites but shorter; speculates this leverage difference could aid throwing or hitting, though no Dominican-specific data exists.

## Evidence cited

## Open questions
- The anthropometric advantage claim is speculative: it extrapolates from broad Black/Asian/Mexican-American comparisons with no Dominican-specific measurements.
- "Excellence breeds excellence... it's science" is asserted as settled from a single general imitation-learning citation, without baseball-specific evidence.
