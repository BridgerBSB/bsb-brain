# Long Vs Short Arm Action | What's The Difference?

## TL;DR
- Arm action length is a tradeoff, not a fault: longer arms generate more potential energy but are harder to time consistently as fatigue sets in; shorter arms repeat more easily but cap out lower and lean on more bicep/arm engagement.
- The real checkpoint isn't length, it's timing: elbow roughly in line with the shoulder near 90° at flip-up, arriving there as the pelvis starts to rotate toward foot plant, not before and not long after.
- Corrections should be sequenced position (mobility) then pattern (slow rep) then power (full speed), and timed to the calendar — off-season for overhauls, in-season only for small, previously-drilled fixes.

## Claims
- [02:03](https://youtu.be/CxObu-w6cJw?t=123) Longer arm action gives more distance to generate force but is harder to time consistently, especially deep into a game as fatigue sets in.
- [03:04](https://youtu.be/CxObu-w6cJw?t=184) Shorter arm action is easier to repeat and get "up on time," but trades away ceiling and tends toward more bicep/arm-muscle engagement ("pushy" delivery).
- [05:05](https://youtu.be/CxObu-w6cJw?t=305) General target at foot plant: the arm should be at least parallel to the ground and starting flip-up, though this varies with each pitcher's shoulder anatomy and range of motion.
- [06:06](https://youtu.be/CxObu-w6cJw?t=366) A shoulder with roughly 30° internal rotation that flows into 90° of layback puts the arm through "over 120°" total rotation (framework credited to coach Ethan Dyer); pitchers with less external rotation should stay less flipped-up at landing.
- [12:16](https://youtu.be/CxObu-w6cJw?t=736) Named example: Misiewicz throws 105 mph with mechanics that look imperfect (high elbow, late flip-up) because his sequencing and timing are elite.
- [13:16](https://youtu.be/CxObu-w6cJw?t=796) Misiewicz's very late arm action in the minor leagues caused repeated injuries; getting him to reach position at landing instead of later let him keep throwing 104 mph, more consistently and more healthily.
- [19:21](https://youtu.be/CxObu-w6cJw?t=1161) Checkpoint used on the plyo wall: elbow in line with the shoulder at flip-up, roughly 90°; guys well outside that range get addressed with drills.
- [26:29](https://youtu.be/CxObu-w6cJw?t=1589) Correction framework ("three P's," credited to Nick Winkelman): position (mobility to access the shape), pattern (can the athlete do it slow), power (can the athlete hold it at full speed) — applied in that order.
- [45:43](https://youtu.be/CxObu-w6cJw?t=2743)–[46:45](https://youtu.be/CxObu-w6cJw?t=2805) Case: an athlete's velo held after an off-season arm-setup change, but command dipped in his first full-speed bullpen of the summer; by fall, after months of reps, command returned alongside the velo gain.
- [55:53](https://youtu.be/CxObu-w6cJw?t=3353) Case: a pro pitcher's fastball started cutting; cueing him to stay stacked over the pelvis instead of getting forward with the torso fixed the cut by the next outing.

## Cues
- **"You need to hold flexion longer"** - fixes early layback/mistimed flip-up; for pitchers with long arm actions ([54:53](https://youtu.be/CxObu-w6cJw?t=3293))
- **"Get it, go. Get it, go."** - three-ball double-knee drill cue; fixes late arm/low elbow by forcing quick flip-up ([24:27](https://youtu.be/CxObu-w6cJw?t=1467))
- **"Stay stacked over the pelvis"** - fixes a cutting fastball caused by getting forward with the torso; used with a pro pitcher ([55:53](https://youtu.be/CxObu-w6cJw?t=3353))

## Evidence cited
- Nikolai Bernstein's blacksmith study: novice blacksmiths repeated the same swing path every time and still mis-hit the target; expert blacksmiths varied their path but hit the same spot, cited as evidence for external over internal focus ([32:32](https://youtu.be/CxObu-w6cJw?t=1952))
- Named anecdote: Lucas Giolito's arm-action change under coach Ethan Katz, prompted by throwing a 2-lb plyo ball that hurt with his old long arm action ([15:18](https://youtu.be/CxObu-w6cJw?t=918))
- Named anecdote: Brock Stewart reportedly worked on his arm path for about a year before it became consistent enough to sustain a big-league career ([41:39](https://youtu.be/CxObu-w6cJw?t=2499))

## Open questions
- The source argues an "inverted W" (elbow above shoulder plane) is only a problem in relation to spine position, not as a standalone shape — this cuts against the common practice of flagging inverted W from video alone, and no injury data is given either way ([16:19](https://youtu.be/CxObu-w6cJw?t=979)).
- Whether long or short arm action is "better" is explicitly left as "it depends," with tradeoffs described from coaching experience rather than measured outcomes (e.g., no injury-rate or command data by arm-action length) ([02:03](https://youtu.be/CxObu-w6cJw?t=123)).
