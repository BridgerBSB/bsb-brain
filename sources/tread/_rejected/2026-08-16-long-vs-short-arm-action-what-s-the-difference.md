# Long Vs Short Arm Action | What's The Difference?

## TL;DR
- Long arm action (hand-driven, less elbow flexion) trades higher potential energy for harder timing consistency; short arm action (elbow-driven, more flexion) trades a lower force ceiling for easier repeatability.
- Target checkpoint is elbow in line with the shoulders at flip-up, roughly 90 degrees, but the coaches explicitly reject a fixed angle like "32 degrees at foot plant" - timing relative to pelvis rotation matters more than hitting a number.
- Mechanical overhauls belong in the off-season; in-season, only repeat quick fixes already proven with that athlete - a genuine change (e.g. Brock Stewart's arm-action rework) can take about a year to hold.

## Claims
- [00:00](https://youtu.be/CxObu-w6cJw?t=0) Longer arm action gives higher potential energy; shorter arm action is easier to be consistent with.
- [01:01](https://youtu.be/CxObu-w6cJw?t=61) Long vs short arm action is defined mainly by elbow flexion through the arm path - long is more hand-driven with less flexion, short is more elbow-driven with more flexion.
- [02:03](https://youtu.be/CxObu-w6cJw?t=123) Long arm action's downside is timing consistency, especially deep into a game as fatigue sets in; short arm action's downside is more bicep/arm-muscle engagement and a lower ceiling for energy generation.
- [05:05](https://youtu.be/CxObu-w6cJw?t=305) General guideline: by front foot plant the arm should be at least above parallel to the ground, starting to flip up - they explicitly reject a fixed checkpoint like "exactly 32 degrees at foot plant" since anatomy (shoulder IR/ER range) varies by athlete.
- [06:06](https://youtu.be/CxObu-w6cJw?t=366) Layback is internal plus external rotation combined, not range in one direction alone (credited to Ethan Dyer) - example given: 30 degrees of IR plus 90 degrees of layback puts the arm over 120 degrees total.
- [13:16](https://youtu.be/CxObu-w6cJw?t=796) Misiewicz throws 104-105 mph with a late-flip arm action; his minor-league injuries came from opening up to slow the arm down, and the fix was better timing at landing, not changing the arm action itself.
- [19:21](https://youtu.be/CxObu-w6cJw?t=1161) Target checkpoint: elbow in line with the shoulders at flip-up, roughly 90 degrees; guys well outside 90 (high or low elbow) get addressed on the plyo wall and in drill work.
- [20:23](https://youtu.be/CxObu-w6cJw?t=1223) Drill: open-hand throws (never closing the hand) for a stabby arm action - dropping the ball forces a tighter, shorter arm path.
- [24:27](https://youtu.be/CxObu-w6cJw?t=1467) Drill: double-knee throws with three balls in hand to force flip-up under a time constraint instead of cueing position directly, used for late/low-elbow arm actions.
- [42:40](https://youtu.be/CxObu-w6cJw?t=2560) Brock Stewart worked on his arm action for about a year before it consistently held - cited as the standard timeframe for a genuine mechanical change to stick.

## Cues
- **"Hold flexion longer"** - fixes early/late layback timing on a long arm action (Misiewicz-style); for pitchers with a longer, later-flipping arm action
- **"Get it, go, get it go"** - fixes a low elbow / late flip-up by forcing the arm up under time pressure rather than cueing the position; for late arm action guys
- **"Be smooth"** - fixes a stabby, yank-then-interrupt arm action without directing the athlete to think about the arm; general use in constrained drills

## Evidence cited
- Nikolai Bernstein blacksmith study: novice smiths repeated the same swing path and mis-hit the stone; expert smiths varied the path but hit the same spot - cited to support external-focus coaching.
- Misiewicz: 104-105 mph with a late-flip, high-elbow arm action; minor-league injury history tied to compensations, resolved by fixing timing rather than the pattern.
- Giolito: switched to a shorter arm action after his old coach (Ethan Katz) had him throw a 2-lb plyo ball, which was uncomfortable with his old long arm action and drove a natural pattern change.
- Brock Stewart: reported working on his arm action for roughly a year before it held consistently.

## Open questions
- The video gives no fixed angle target for arm position at foot plant and argues against one ("not exactly 32 degrees"), which conflicts with more angle-checkpoint-driven coaching approaches.
- The in-season vs off-season change guidance (small proven fixes only in-season, overhauls only in the off-season) is asserted from experience and anecdote, without a cited study or tracked outcomes.
