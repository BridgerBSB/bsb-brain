# Brusdar Graterol Pitching Mechanics | 100 MPH Sinker

## TL;DR
- Ben Brewster breaks down Graterol's lower half in three phases (drift, hinge, rotation into landing) and shows what efficient hard throwers share.
- Key numbers: roughly 4 to 6 inches of center-of-mass drift at peak leg lift, pelvis hike no more than 10 to 15 degrees, about 90 degrees of elbow flexion at landing, 45 to 55 degrees of pelvic rotation at landing.
- Small flaws he lists for a Graterol-type mover: a short hinge, limited back-foot eversion, and an arm that is sometimes late at landing.

## Claims
- [01:02](https://youtu.be/xsV_4qvUcY4?t=62) Graterol is 22, about 67% sinkers and 29% sliders, 99 mph average, 15.75 in horizontal and 7.27 in vertical movement.
- [02:04](https://youtu.be/xsV_4qvUcY4?t=124) The drift is a forward shift of center of mass (belly button) during leg lift. There are only so many efficient ways to throw 100, so hard throwers share patterns.
- [03:05](https://youtu.be/xsV_4qvUcY4?t=185) At peak leg lift his center shifts about 4 to 6 inches, paired with a posterior pelvic tilt that coils over the back hip.
- [05:06](https://youtu.be/xsV_4qvUcY4?t=306) The lower half has three phases: drift, hinge (linear move), rotation into landing. He hinges in a high internal-rotation position on the back hip; Verlander and Kluber use a vertical shin with neutral or slightly external rotation. The best position depends on hip anatomy.
- [07:08](https://youtu.be/xsV_4qvUcY4?t=428) Higher-level throwers keep the pelvis stacked, hiking no more than about 10 to 15 degrees above horizon. Lower-level throwers stay back too far and hike steeply uphill.
- [07:08](https://youtu.be/xsV_4qvUcY4?t=428) At landing the elbow is in line with the shoulders at roughly 90 degrees of flexion, which gets the ball into the plane of rotation. His is almost exactly 90.
- [09:12](https://youtu.be/xsV_4qvUcY4?t=552) Hard throwers do not land square: the pelvis corkscrews open late with shoulders closed, about 45 to 55 degrees open at landing. The front knee then extends because the finishing pelvic rotation pulls the femur, not from quad extension ([10:12](https://youtu.be/xsV_4qvUcY4?t=612)).
- [10:12](https://youtu.be/xsV_4qvUcY4?t=612) A healthy follow-through unwinds: forearm pronates, shoulder internally rotates, scap protracts, T-spine rotates. His recoil is aggressive. One athlete who felt he decelerated into release gained velocity when cued "slap yourself on the back"; Brewster does not recommend testing it broadly.
- [12:13](https://youtu.be/xsV_4qvUcY4?t=733) Possible gains: ride the linear move longer, since he pops out a hair early, and let the back foot evert and roll over instead of pushing off early ([13:14](https://youtu.be/xsV_4qvUcY4?t=794)). Brewster calls both small.
- [14:16](https://youtu.be/xsV_4qvUcY4?t=856) His arm is sometimes late at landing, a repeatability issue. A late arm with violent rotation can add sink and run, and he still threw 100 with 15+ in of horizontal movement.
