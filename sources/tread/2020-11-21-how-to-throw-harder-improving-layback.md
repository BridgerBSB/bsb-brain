# How to Throw Harder | Improving Layback

## TL;DR
- Layback is the sum of T-spine extension, scapular posterior tilt, clavicle rotation and true shoulder external rotation, not shoulder ER alone.
- About 180 degrees (plus or minus 15) is the big-league range; 120 degrees is a real limiter for throwing 95+.
- Improve it without stretching into ER: tissue and active mobility work, then fix arm timing, arm plane and posture.

## Claims
- [00:00](https://youtu.be/mBlogIlktXY?t=0) Layback is not shoulder external rotation. Numbers like 180 degrees include T-spine, scapula and clavicle; true ball-and-socket ER never approaches 180.
- [02:02](https://youtu.be/mBlogIlktXY?t=122) T-spine extension versus flexion changes layback by 15 to 25 degrees depending on the athlete. Scapular posterior tilt and clavicle rotation also contribute.
- [05:07](https://youtu.be/mBlogIlktXY?t=307) More layback is not always better. Roughly 180 degrees plus or minus 15 is typical of hard throwers; more range from the joint itself costs stability.
- [07:10](https://youtu.be/mBlogIlktXY?t=430) Bone structure (acromion type), humeral retroversion (about 5 to 10 degrees, from throwing before growth plates close) and congenital laxity set a ceiling.
- [10:12](https://youtu.be/mBlogIlktXY?t=612) Extremely limited layback (120 degrees) makes 95+ very difficult. Excess (200+ degrees) is rare and can cause velocity fluctuation and a dragged arm.
- [11:12](https://youtu.be/mBlogIlktXY?t=672) Stretching into external rotation is generally ill-advised in the therapy world; the shoulder is already unstable. Not black and white.
- [12:14](https://youtu.be/mBlogIlktXY?t=734) A sudden loss of prior layback, especially after elbow or shoulder injury, may be a protective mechanism. Do not force through compensations; use a pseudo-throwing phase before throwing.
- [18:45](https://youtu.be/mBlogIlktXY?t=1125) Tissue work targets pecs major and minor, subclavius, coracobrachialis and posterior cuff for extensibility; added range is secondary. Active stretching (contract-relax, FRC, PAILs/RAILs, extreme isos) builds strength at end range.
- [24:01](https://youtu.be/mBlogIlktXY?t=1441) Mechanical causes of limited layback: arm not up at landing (late flip-up leaves one frame to show layback), elbow low or high of the shoulder line, linear forward-crunch follow-through, early torso rotation.
- [28:20](https://youtu.be/mBlogIlktXY?t=1700) Tipping forward (toe and quad driven) loses T-spine extension and layback. Fake layback is a climbing elbow; true layback has the elbow behind the ear (at least the nose) and in line with the shoulders.

## Drills
- **Pseudo-throwing med ball phase** - med ball trampoline rebounders, internal rotation tosses, wall taps, push passes before any throwing; builds tolerance to valgus stress and torque without full layback; for return-to-throw after elbow or shoulder injury

## Evidence cited
- Study of professional pitchers over a season: about 1.5 degrees gain in shoulder ER (study not named).
- Six-week weighted ball study: 3.3 degrees ER gain; speaker notes it was highly criticized.
- Billy Wagner (near 200 degrees) and Matt Harvey (about 165 to 170) as range examples.
- TJ Antone breakdown: timing fix showed more layback and a velocity bump the next year (no numbers).
- Coach Paul: mid 90s to upper 90s with posture changes plus training changes.

## Open questions
- The 180 degrees plus or minus 15 optimum and the 15 to 25 degree T-spine contribution are asserted without a cited dataset or measurement method.
- Active stretching and tissue work are recommended for layback, but no outcome data is given; the speaker admits measurement varies between testers.
