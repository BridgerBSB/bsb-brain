# The Case for Lifting Weights, A Declaration for The Youth Pitcher

## TL;DR
- Argues that lean body mass, not mechanics, is the main lever the average pitcher has for gaining velocity.
- Cites average MLB fastball velocity rising from 89.2 mph (1990) to 93.3 mph (2017) alongside average player bodyweight rising from 191 to 213 lbs over the same span.
- Claims a post-pubescent novice can add up to 20 lbs of lean mass in one year of dedicated training and nutrition, moving about 1.5 standard deviations up the velocity curve.

## Claims
- Acceleration and velocity: F = ma, with an exponential relationship between velocity and force; most 95+ mph throwers rely on mass, needing roughly 175-180+ lbs of lean body mass rather than arm/shoulder gifts alone.
- Average Bodyweight of Major League Players: average MLB bodyweight rose from ~191 lbs (1990) to ~213 lbs (2017); average four-seam velocity rose from 89.2 mph to 93.3 mph over the same period (source: MLB.com, FanGraphs.com).
- Average Bodyweight of Major League Players: a 90 mph left-handed pitcher who would have gone in the top 3 rounds in 1990 now gets only college interest or a late-round pick.
- Visually, think of it this way: average high school fastball velocity sits near 76-84 mph, tracking a lean body mass distribution centered near the population mean.
- How fast can you move toward the right of the curve?: a post-pubescent novice can gain up to 20 lbs of lean mass within one year of a structured surplus-diet and training program, a ~1.5 SD move in lean body mass matched to a similar move in velocity.
- How fast can you move toward the right of the curve?: example given is a 16-year-old starting at 130 lbs reaching 150 lbs of lean mass in 12-18 months.
- Other Implications: average lean body mass is cited as ~120 lbs at 13 years old, reaching ~165-170 lbs by 17-18, comparable to a Division I college player.
- Closing Thoughts: taller pitchers gain roughly 8-10 lbs of lean mass per inch of height, which the author says explains scouting "projectability" more than any inherent mechanical advantage.

## Evidence cited
- MLB.com and FanGraphs.com data: average MLB player bodyweight 191 lbs (1990) to 213 lbs (2017); average four-seam velocity 89.2 mph to 93.3 mph.
- Editor's note: the author documented his own lean body mass progression through high school and college as a case study, showing diminishing velocity returns as training age advanced.
- Sammy Sosa and Mark McGwire's 1998 home run chase power increase is attributed to each carrying 25-30 lbs more muscle mass than as rookies, noted alongside their later steroid admissions.

## Open questions
- The article treats lean mass gain as the primary driver of the 1990-2017 MLB velocity increase without addressing how much of that trend could reflect throwing programs, biomechanics coaching, or selection effects at the pro level.
- The claim that a novice's 1.5 SD gain in lean body mass converts to roughly 1.5 SD of velocity gain is asserted without a cited study linking the two at that ratio.
