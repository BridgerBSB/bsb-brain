# Recovery From Training: Part One

## TL;DR
- Athletes get stronger when they recover, not when they train. Good programs overload and disrupt homeostasis, but do not overtrain.
- Heavy lifting temporarily lowers pitching ability, and pitching while fatigued changes mechanics and raises injury risk.
- Novices can lift heavy and throw bullpens with little recovery management. Intermediates and advanced athletes need modulated intensity so they throw max-effort bullpens at 90-95% fresh.

## Claims
- Intro: Selye's General Adaptation Syndrome is the adaptation model used in training. It has three stages: alarm, resistance, exhaustion.
- Intro, Alarm stage: any stress that drives adaptation, such as a harder set of squats or a longer run, triggers the fight-or-flight response.
- Intro, Resistance stage: if the stress was enough to drive adaptation, fitness is temporarily lower. A new squat 5RM on Monday leaves squat strength significantly diminished on Tuesday.
- Intro, Exhaustion stage: prolonged stress beyond the body's ability to adapt is the overtrained state.
- Intro: novices (often high school athletes) are unadapted to stress and can be pushed to their limit every session. Overloading disrupts homeostasis; overtraining ignores the time needed to recover from it.
- What Does This Mean for Baseball Players: overtraining matters most for people who train seriously, and pitchers in particular. Pitching is anaerobic/ballistic, so lost long-slow-distance endurance barely affects it.
- What Does This Mean for Baseball Players: in the example, a pitcher who sits at 80 mph rested (best 83 mph) will throw under 80 after a heavy squat 5RM the day before. The author calls the dip a good thing because it signals the stress that will improve him once recovered, but says throwing that bullpen is probably not a good idea.
- What Does This Mean for Baseball Players: pitching while fatigued is a good way to get hurt. Fatigued legs and core can leave the arm lagging, which creates a flaw in arm cocking.
- How Do You Manage Recovery: novices can squat, lift heavy and sprint while throwing bullpens with few or no detrimental effects. Recovery planning becomes a real issue as players move toward the advanced end in both pitching and lifting.
- How Do You Manage Recovery: for intermediate and advanced athletes, break 5RM/3RM/1RM attempts into longer intervals and add more assistance, accessory and partial work, so bullpens happen at 90-95% fresh. Novices and early intermediates do not need this complexity and should focus on getting strong and sticking to the program.

## Evidence cited
- Hans Selye's General Adaptation Syndrome framework.
- Mark Rippetoe, Practical Programming for Strength Training (recommended reading).
- Author's own novice period: about 8 months, +250 lb back squat, +100 lb bench press, +300 lb deadlift, bodyweight maintained.
- Two earlier Driveline posts, on overtraining and on energy system development, are linked but not summarised here.

## Open questions
- The 80 mph/83 mph example and the 90-95% fresh target are illustrative, with no data or study behind them.
- The author says assistance work is wasted on novices, which may conflict with sources that program accessory work from the start. The gains cited are one person's novice period.
