# "What's a good workout, Kyle?"

## TL;DR
- Argues free, generic workout plans fail because recipients quit without financial or in-person commitment; paid or in-gym clients follow through.
- States one non-negotiable training element: some form of squatting pattern belongs in every workout.
- Uses illustrative (not researched) numbers on opposing talent (450 lb squat, 200 ground balls a week, 88/72 mph pitch pairing) to argue development takes sustained hard work, not shortcuts.

## Claims
- "What's a good workout, Kyle?": Free workout plans given by email usually fail; recipients report the plan was "too hard" or they "fell off the wagon," so the author stopped giving them away.
- "What's a good workout, Kyle?": Paying for training or training in person increases follow-through compared with getting advice for free.
- "What's a good workout, Kyle?": Running Driveline Baseball, Inc. is not the author's primary money-maker; his stated motivation is coaching athletes who commit to the work.
- "What's a good workout, Kyle?": Frames the standard for hitters as facing a 6'1", 225 lb opponent who has squatted since November and can handle 450 lbs explosively.
- "What's a good workout, Kyle?": Frames the standard for infielders as a 195 lb shortstop taking 200 ground balls a week since the prior season ended.
- "What's a good workout, Kyle?": Frames the standard for pitchers as an 88 mph fastball paired with a 72 mph curveball spotted on the outside corner, a location the pitcher had worked on for 6 months.
- "What's a good workout, Kyle?": States that some form of squatting pattern is required in a workout; without it, "just stop working out entirely."
