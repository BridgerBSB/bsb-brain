# Study: Strength Training Make You a Better Baseball Player (really?)

## TL;DR
- A 37-player NCAA D1 correlation study found bat velocity tracked most closely with vertical jump (r = 0.58), then medicine ball side toss (r = 0.50) and lean body mass (r = 0.43).
- The study's own conclusion: strength programs for baseball should emphasize leg power, rotational power and lean body mass.
- The post is Driveline arguing against local facilities that discourage weightlifting for youth, and it ends as a sales pitch.

## Claims
- Intro: A local Seattle facility said weight and power lifting are "discouraged by medical and fitness professionals" for young athletes. The author calls this false.
- Relative Safety of Weightlifting for Youth (quoted from StrongKid.com, Avery Faigenbaum): the belief that properly performed weightlifting is riskier than other sports and activities "is not consistent with research findings."
- Study methods: 37 NCAA D1 baseball players, mean age 19.7, tested on body fat, LBM, grip, 1RM bench, 1-arm DB row, 1RM squat, med ball side toss, vertical jump, 10/30/60 yd sprints, throwing velocity, bat velocity and batted-ball velocity.
- Study results: bat velocity vs batted-ball velocity r = 0.70 (r2 = 0.49); bat velocity vs vertical jump r = 0.58 (r2 = 0.34); vs lean body mass r = 0.43 (r2 = 0.18).
- Study results: med ball side toss vs bat velocity r = 0.50 (r2 = 0.25), vs throwing velocity r = 0.49 (r2 = 0.24), vs batted-ball velocity r = 0.45 (r2 = 0.20).
- Delving Into the Specifics: vertical jump had the highest correlation with bat velocity, which the author takes as evidence against dismissing jump training in the name of "sport-specificity."
- Delving Into the Specifics: 1RM squat was not significantly linked to any other variable. The author suspects the squats were above parallel or on a Smith machine, but says the study did not describe the method.
- Humorous Use of This Study: Dick Mills quoted the study's implication line to argue strength training has little predictive value. The author says Mills left out the conclusion, which says the opposite.
- Humorous Use of This Study: ASMI researcher Dave Fortenbaugh is quoted that an exercise explaining 20 to 30 percent of the variance in bat velocity is worth spending effort on.
- What It Means at Driveline: the author says Driveline's training agrees with published sport science and that many local facilities do not.

## Evidence cited
- ACSM Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise 2009 abstract, "The Relationship Between Power and Lean Body Mass": n = 37 NCAA D1 baseball players, correlation matrix, p < 0.05.

## Open questions
- The squat explanation (shallow depth or Smith machine) is the author's guess. The study did not report squat method, and a correlation in 37 players does not show that training vertical jump raises bat speed.
- The post argues from correlation and from facility rivalry. It gives no training-intervention data for the "weightlifting is safe for youth" claim beyond the quoted StrongKid statement.
