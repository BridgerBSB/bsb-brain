# P90x for Baseball?

## TL;DR
- Argues against P90X-style "muscle confusion" programs for baseball athletes because constantly rotating exercises makes progress untrackable and delivers too little heavy loading to build real strength.
- Isolation-based, body-part-split training has little carryover to a compound, rotational sport like baseball, and training hard six days a week raises overtraining and injury risk in a population where, per Eric Cressey, 57% of pitchers already report some shoulder injury each season.
- Recommends untrained novices start with a compound-lift program like Starting Strength, with pitchers modifying overhead pressing and swapping the low-bar back squat for a front squat or high-bar back squat.

## Claims
- (Eric Cressey, quoted in intro) - 57% of pitchers suffer some sort of shoulder injury each season, cited as a reason to prioritize safe, non-fatiguing technique over variety.
- (Problem One) - Constantly introducing new moves and routines daily prevents accurately tracking progress through a program.
- (Problem Two) - P90X uses little to no heavy resistance training; real strength requires compound movements like the squat, deadlift, bench press, press, and rows.
- (Problem Three) - Training hard six days a week with a single light-recovery day (yoga/stretching) leads to overtraining.
- (Problem Four) - Isolation-based, body-part-split training (e.g., "Chest and Back" one day, "Shoulders and Arms" another) has little carryover to athletic competition, which relies on compound, multi-joint movement.
- (Three tenets of exercise science) - "Everything works, some things work better than others, nothing works forever": an untrained person running 2 miles every other day will raise their squat 1RM, but that doesn't make running the best way to build squat strength.
- (Three tenets of exercise science) - Illustrates the third tenet with Olympic lifters needing roughly four years to raise a Clean and Jerk from 212 kg to 214 kg, showing how much training response slows with training age.
- (Recommendation) - For untrained novices, recommends Starting Strength (Rippetoe and Kilgore); for baseball pitchers specifically, advises against overhead pressing and suggests substituting a front squat or high-bar back squat for the low-bar back squat.
