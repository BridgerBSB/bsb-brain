# Kinematic Analysis: Wrist to Elbow Relationship

## TL;DR
- In the late-cocking phase, the shoulder rotates up to 937 degrees/second and the elbow up to 1160 degrees/second, generating the forces behind elbow torque.
- Driveline tracks the pitching wrist and elbow frame by frame (0.00476s intervals) to see how fast the forearm lays back into max shoulder external rotation, treating a rapid elbow-outpaces-wrist pattern as a timing flaw.
- The method is illustrated with a single anonymous youth pitcher, not a validated study, and commenters directly challenge the math behind the delta calculation.

## Claims
- Article body: during late-cocking, the shoulder rotates up to 937 degrees/second and the elbow up to 1160 degrees/second.
- Article body: the trajectory analysis samples frames at 0.00476 second intervals and tracks wrist and elbow pixel position, plus the delta and delta-of-delta between frames.
- Article body: the example pitcher is an anonymous youth pitcher evaluated with high-speed footage taken within the past month.
- Article body: when the elbow trajectory rapidly outpaces the wrist, the forearm lays back violently behind the elbow into max external rotation, which the author frames as a timing flaw that can be reduced with training and mechanical changes.
- Comments: readers argue the delta-of-deltas column should not use absolute value, since a signed value would show which joint (wrist or elbow) is accelerating faster, and absolute value "blurs the wrist/elbow relationship."
- Comments: the author concedes the final column "isn't necessarily the correct thing to measure" and agrees sign matters, particularly negative values showing the wrist moving slower or backward.

## Evidence cited
- One anonymous youth pitcher's high-speed trajectory chart (wrist and elbow pixel path over time); no named athlete outcomes, no published study or sample size beyond this single case.

## Open questions
- The comment thread disputes the core methodology (absolute value vs. signed delta-of-deltas) and the author agrees the original approach may be wrong, but the article itself was never revised to reflect this.
- The article states "not every pitcher should be within a given range" for wrist/elbow delta sums without stating what that range is or citing evidence for it.
