# The Fall Ball Decision: Which of These 3 Pitchers Is YOUR Kid?

## TL;DR
- Games test what a pitcher already has; training builds what they don't. The fall ball decision should follow from which one a player needs, not from a registration deadline.
- Three youth pitcher types get different recommendations: the overused grinder and the dominant same-age arm should both rest then train, the underused arm should play for innings.
- Driveline's internal sample of roughly 1,700 pitchers ages 12-18 shows velocity climbing from about 60 mph at 12 to about 79 mph by high school sophomore year, and players who train through an off-season gain about 2.5 mph on average.

## Claims
- [00:00](https://youtu.be/jGwwwoAaRRs?t=0) Games test what a player already has; training changes what they bring to that test.
- [01:02](https://youtu.be/jGwwwoAaRRs?t=62) The real question after a season isn't whether fall ball is good, it's whether the player needs more of the same testing (games) or more size, strength, and skill (training).
- [01:02](https://youtu.be/jGwwwoAaRRs?t=62) Type 1, the overused arm who threw heavy innings and got outs via location and defense rather than swing-and-miss stuff, should rest then train on stuff, not throw more innings.
- [02:07](https://youtu.be/jGwwwoAaRRs?t=127) Type 2, the dominant arm who is already the best pitcher on the team, should also rest and train, since skills built beating 12-14 year olds don't automatically survive the jump to a high school mound against 17-18 year olds.
- [04:10](https://youtu.be/jGwwwoAaRRs?t=250) Type 3, the underused arm who threw only 6-14 innings all summer, needs more game innings, since a bullpen can't replicate the experience of pitching in a close game's sixth inning.
- [04:10](https://youtu.be/jGwwwoAaRRs?t=250) Driveline has measured throwing velocity on roughly 1,700 pitchers ages 12-18: about 60 mph at 12, 65 at 13, 72 at 14, and about 79 mph for a high school sophomore.
- [05:13](https://youtu.be/jGwwwoAaRRs?t=313) A typical 13-year-old has about 14 mph to find over roughly two years to reach the 79 mph sophomore benchmark, and that 79 mph is described as the middle of a high school roster, not a recruitable-track number.
- [05:13](https://youtu.be/jGwwwoAaRRs?t=313) Among players who trained through fall and winter, the typical player gained about 2.5 mph, and the top quarter gained more than 4 mph.
- [05:13](https://youtu.be/jGwwwoAaRRs?t=313) The number of fall/winter off-seasons left before high school should shape the decision: three if the player is 12, two if 13, one if 14.
- [07:20](https://youtu.be/jGwwwoAaRRs?t=440) Keeping the player's love of the game intact is framed as more important than the training math, since burnout undermines everything else.

## Evidence cited
- Driveline internal velocity dataset: roughly 1,700 pitchers ages 12-18, with age-based velocity benchmarks and average/top-quartile off-season gain figures cited at [04:10](https://youtu.be/jGwwwoAaRRs?t=250) and [05:13](https://youtu.be/jGwwwoAaRRs?t=313).

## Open questions
- The recommendation to rest and train the "dominant" pitcher (type 2) is asserted qualitatively, without data showing that same-age dominance actually fails to transfer to older competition.
- The video doesn't state whether the 2.5 mph average / 4+ mph top-quartile gain figures come from the same 12-18 sample used for the benchmark numbers, leaving the mapping between the two datasets unclear.
