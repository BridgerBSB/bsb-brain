# Our Influences - Pitching

## TL;DR
- Driveline states it belongs to no single pitching "camp," drawing ideas from multiple researchers and coaches rather than adopting any one system wholesale.
- Even from a source it calls "controversial" (Mike Marshall), Driveline kept specific implements (wrist weights) and some mechanical theory without adopting his full method.
- No instruction, cues, or drills are given; this is a positioning piece explaining why Driveline's pitching programming mixes influences.

## Claims
- Pitching: Driveline says it does not belong to one "camp" for pitching, hitting, or strength philosophy, and instead studies people who have done their own research and experimentation.
- Pitching: Names the National Pitching Association (NPA) as an influence; the author completed the NPA's online pitching mechanics course and cites its book The Art and Science of Pitching and instructor Tom House.
- Pitching: Names Dr. Mike Marshall as an influence while calling him "controversial" and disagreeing with his "dogmatic" approach; Marshall's system has pitchers face the batter, step straight forward, and forcefully pronate through release, trained with heavy wrist weights, lead balls, and plastic javelins.
- Pitching: States Driveline uses some of Marshall's conditioning implements (wrist weights) and believes some of his mechanical theories "have solid merit," without adopting his full system.
- Pitching: Names Eric Cressey of Cressey Performance as an influence, citing his MS in Exercise Science, background as a former powerlifter, his book Maximum Strength, and blog writing on self-myofascial release, deadlifting, and band work for pitchers.
- Pitching: States ASMI was deliberately left off the influences list, despite being called "a big one," because the author judged it "wasn't applicable as an 'influence'" in the same sense as the others.
- 1 comment: A reader states pronation performed too early in the delivery reduces release velocity, but pronation held back until just before release and driven through forcefully can add velocity, calling it "an acquired skill."
