# Basic Baseball Supplements

## TL;DR
- The article gives a three-part filter for baseball supplements (better than the whole food source, cost-effective, evidence-backed) and uses it to reject nitric-oxide "pump" products outright.
- Only four supplements clear the bar: creatine monohydrate (3-5 g/day), fish oil (1-2 g EPA/DHA/day), a whey protein powder, and a generic multivitamin.
- Creatine is framed as the highest-value pick, backed by a placebo-controlled 1RM study and costing about 12.5 cents/day at the recommended dose.

## Claims
- **Safe, Legal, Effective - The Three Part Test for Baseball Supplements**: a supplement should pass three filters: demonstrably better than the whole food source, cost-effective, and backed by real science; "NO Explode" type nitric-oxide/pump products are named as an example that fails all three.
- **Safe, Legal, Effective - The Three Part Test for Baseball Supplements**: cites Eric Cressey (*Maximum Strength*) that arginine supplementation can mute the growth hormone response to resistance training rather than augment strength gains.
- **Creatine Monohydrate**: recommends 3-5 g/day of creatine monohydrate, no loading phase, optionally split morning/night; cites a Journal of Athletic Training source showing creatine improves one-rep max versus placebo.
- **Creatine Monohydrate**: a cited 7-week study (Spillane) on creatine ethyl ester (CEE) in non-resistance-trained males found no additional strength, muscle-mass, or creatine-retention benefit over creatine monohydrate or placebo.
- **Creatine Monohydrate**: at $25.99 for a 1200 g tub and 5 g/day dosing, cost works out to about 12.5 cents/day over a 240-day supply.
- **Fish Oil - EPA/DHA Supplements**: recommends 1-2 g of combined EPA/DHA per day for inflammation reduction, blood pressure, and triglycerides; since 1000 mg capsules typically contain only 300 mg combined EPA/DHA, that means 3-6 capsules/day (2 per meal).
- **Protein Powders**: recommends Optimum Nutrition Gold Standard whey, reconstituted with milk post-workout; framed strictly as a supplement to food, not a replacement, and notes shakes already supply enough arginine/glutamine that separate glutamine supplementation is unnecessary.
- **A Generic Multivitamin**: recommends one generic multivitamin per day with dinner (up to two), costing about $18 for a bottle of 500.

## Evidence cited
- Journal of Athletic Training source cited in **Creatine Monohydrate**: creatine monohydrate improves one-rep max versus placebo.
- Spillane et al., 7-week study cited in **Creatine Monohydrate**: creatine ethyl ester (CEE) vs. creatine monohydrate vs. placebo in non-resistance-trained males; no additional benefit from CEE in strength, muscle mass, or creatine retention.
- Eric Cressey, *Maximum Strength*, quoted in **Safe, Legal, Effective - The Three Part Test for Baseball Supplements**: arginine claims lack scientific basis and may blunt the growth hormone response to training.

## Open questions
- The CEE finding is generalized from a single 7-week trial in non-resistance-trained males, but the article presents it as a settled comparison to creatine monohydrate for all trainees.
- The claim that arginine supplementation mutes the growth hormone response is sourced to a secondary quote (Cressey's book) rather than a study cited directly in the article.
